# Reversal / dispute / chargeback authorization patterns in real banking (T-019)

**Task:** T-019. **Status:** Research only — no code, no design decision. This exists to inform the owner's eventual design of IdentityApi's reversal authority (T-015), which `research/identityapi-design-review.md` flagged as the single item most clearly tied to real financial-loss risk if left vague, and which the owner deliberately deferred rather than decide quickly.

**What this builds on, and does not re-litigate:** `ARCHITECTURE.md` Section 9 (decision D-7, 16 Oct 2026) and the IdentityApi design draft's own Section 9 item 8 have already fixed the **ledger mechanics**: a chain reversal undoes every leg, last leg first, in one database transaction, all-or-nothing — if any leg cannot be reversed (e.g. the recipient already spent the money), the **whole** reversal fails and nothing is refunded, with no remediation path built yet. Reversing a single leg of a chain alone is rejected (422). None of that is revisited here. This brief is about everything **above** the ledger: who may ask for a reversal, what has to be true before IdentityApi lets the request through, what reason the request carries, and what happens operationally once the ledger says "can't do it." It also builds on, and does not repeat, two existing GARBATI research briefs: `research/nigerian-regulatory-requirements.md` (CBN/Sharia regulatory landscape) and `research/joint-account-approval-patterns.md` (maker-checker/M-of-N mechanics for the *original* transaction's multi-holder approval).

## Plain-language summary (read this first)

A few terms, defined once:

- **Chargeback**: a *card-network* process where a cardholder's bank forcibly pulls money back from a merchant's bank, outside the merchant's control, following a formal dispute. It is one specific kind of reversal, not a synonym for all of them.
- **Reversal**: the general term used in this brief (and in GARBATI's own `ARCHITECTURE.md`) for any transaction that undoes or offsets an earlier one. A chargeback, an ACH reversal, a bank's own error-correction, and a customer-initiated refund request are all *kinds* of reversal.
- **Write-off (also "charge-off")**: an *accounting* event where an institution declares a debt or loss unrecoverable and removes it from the books as an expense. It does not erase the underlying obligation or forgive the debtor legally — it is a bookkeeping and loss-recognition act, separate from any later collection effort.
- **Maker-checker (reused from `research/joint-account-approval-patterns.md`)**: the banking rule that one person (the "maker") requests or creates an action and a **different** person (the "checker") must approve it before it takes effect; the maker may not also be the checker of their own request.
- **Reason code**: a short, standardized code attached to a payment message or dispute that states *why* it is being returned, recalled or disputed (e.g. "duplicate," "fraud," "insufficient funds") — used so that systems and institutions on both sides of a transfer can process the return without a human reading free-text explanations every time.

**Short answer to the five questions, in one paragraph each:** (1) Who initiates depends heavily on *whose error it is* — an institution reverses its own processing mistakes (duplicate send, wrong amount, technical failure) unilaterally and fast, while a customer who disputes someone else's action (fraud, goods not received, billing error) must file a claim that the institution then investigates; a bounced/failed settlement is typically auto-reversed by the clearing system itself, no human request needed. (2) Approval scales by amount/risk the same way GARBATI's maker-checker pattern already works elsewhere (a "checker" role confirms the reversal, with escalation for unusual/high-value cases) — but I found no standard source that directly says whether reversing a *joint-account* transaction needs the same multi-holder consent the original transaction needed; this is flagged as an open gap, not answered. (3) Yes — ISO 20022 (`camt.056`/`pacs.004`/SEPA) and the card networks (Visa, Mastercard) each already publish structured reason-code sets with the same handful of underlying categories: duplicate, fraud, technical/processing error, customer dispute, insufficient funds, closed/invalid account, and "customer requested." (4) When the money truly cannot be clawed back, real institutions distinguish a **recoverable** claim (lien, watch-list, collections, legal action against the recipient) from a **loss to absorb** (a formal write-off/charge-off, sometimes offset later by insurance/fidelity-bond recovery or regulatory loss-reporting) — the switch from one to the other is a deliberate, time-bound decision, not automatic. (5) Yes, clearly: a bank correcting its own error typically has a *short, fixed* window (hours to a few banking days) and reverses unilaterally; a customer dispute typically gets a *longer, investigation-bound* window (days to months) with required provisional-credit and notification steps; Nigeria's CBN has published its own concrete versions of both patterns (see Q5 and cross-reference `research/nigerian-regulatory-requirements.md`).

## Q1: Who initiates a reversal, in practice?

**Confidence: high** — consistent, convergent evidence across multiple independent payment-rail sources.

Real systems split clearly along *whose mistake or claim it is*, not along a single "customer vs. bank" axis:

- **The institution's own error — unilateral, bank-initiated, no customer request needed.** NACHA's ACH Operating Rules let an Originator or its bank (the ODFI) **unilaterally** reverse an entry it sent by mistake — duplicate send, wrong receiver, wrong dollar amount, or certain timing errors — by transmitting a Reversing Entry within **5 banking days** of the original Settlement Date. The fields (amount, company ID, SEC code) must match the original exactly, and the originator must notify the receiver of the reversal and its reason by the reversal's settlement date. [NACHA — ACH Network Rules: Reversals and Enforcement](https://www.nacha.org/rules/reversals-and-enforcement). The ISO 20022 `camt.056` payment-cancellation-request message exists for the same purpose on the ISO 20022 rails — a sending bank recalls a payment it sent in error, using exactly three standard reasons: `DUPL` (duplicate), `TECH` (technical problem), `FRAD` (fraudulent origin) — i.e. the bank's-own-error recall is itself a narrower, specifically-named reason set than the full customer-dispute reason list (Q3). [SEPA/ISO 20022 reason codes — CPG glossary](https://www.cpg.de/en/glossary/overview-sepa-reason-codes/).
- **Customer disputing someone else's action — customer-initiated claim, then investigated.** A chargeback is specifically described as cardholder-initiated: "a consumer may initiate a chargeback by contacting their issuing bank and filing a substantiated complaint." [Chargeback Gurus — The Complete Guide to the Chargeback Process](https://www.chargebackgurus.com/blog/chargeback-process); [Wikipedia — Chargeback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chargeback). The same split exists under US consumer-protection law: Regulation E requires the *consumer* to notify the institution of a suspected unauthorized transfer before the institution's investigation clock starts — the institution does not act on its own initiative for this category. [eCFR — 12 CFR Part 1005 (Regulation E)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/chapter-X/part-1005); [Canarie — Regulation E Error Resolution Timeframes](https://www.canarie.ai/blog/regulation-e-error-resolution-timeframes).
- **Merchant-initiated reversal of its own transaction = a "refund," a distinct third category**, not a chargeback: "a refund is when the business returns funds to the customer, whereas a chargeback is when the customer's bank... pulls back the funds from the business" without the merchant's cooperation. [Chargeback.io — Chargeback vs. Refund](https://www.chargeback.io/blog/chargeback-vs-refund).
- **Failed/bounced settlement — fully automated, no human request.** CBN's own rule for a failed "on-us" ATM transaction in Nigeria is that it "shall be instantly reversed," with manual reversal only as a fallback if the automatic one fails — i.e. the system itself initiates and completes the reversal with no customer dispute step at all. [CBN circular on electronic-channel dispense errors/refund timelines, via Proshare summary — see Q5 for full detail]. NACHA's same reversal-initiation framework for the originator's own technical/duplicate/wrong-amount error is the ACH-rail analogue of this — detected by the originator or its bank, not claimed by the receiver.
- **A separate, explicitly-defined category for a non-error, non-fraud, "take it back" request**: ISO 20022 defines a distinct Return-reason/Recall-request code `CUST` ("customer requested") for when the *originating* customer simply wants a payment pulled back with no claim of error or fraud — this sits apart from both the bank's-own-error codes (`DUPL`/`TECH`/`FRAD`) and the formal dispute machinery, confirming that "sender changed their mind" is its own named initiation path in the standard, distinct from "sender's bank made a mistake" and from "receiver's customer is disputing something." [SEPA/ISO 20022 reason codes — CPG glossary](https://www.cpg.de/en/glossary/overview-sepa-reason-codes/).

**What I could not verify:** no single source states a general cross-domain rule for "who may initiate a reversal" as a unified taxonomy — the categorization above (bank's-own-error / customer-dispute / merchant-refund / automated-failed-settlement / sender-changed-mind) is this researcher's synthesis across several independently-documented rail-specific rules, not a claim made by any one cited source.

## Q2: What approval is required, and does it scale with risk? (including joint-account consent)

**Confidence: medium** on general maker-checker/risk-scaling (several independent sources, same shape as GARBATI's existing pattern); **low, a real gap** on the specific joint-account-reversal-consent question — flagged explicitly below.

- **General risk-scaled approval.** Core-banking operational guidance describes the same maker-checker pattern already used elsewhere in GARBATI's design applied specifically to back-dated/back-value entries and reversals: "dual control (maker/checker) shall be required to make any back value entry," with posting limits for the maker/teller and separate authorization limits for the checker, and an escalation/"reviewer" role for "unusual values, threshold breaches, and unresolved disagreements" who must not also be the maker or checker on the same record. [Bangladesh Bank — Core Banking Solution (CBS) Features and Controls, BRPD circular](https://www.bb.org.bd/mediaroom/circulars/brpd/may132024brpd12e.pdf); [general maker-checker/dual-approval descriptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maker-checker). This matches `research/joint-account-approval-patterns.md`'s own finding that an unweighted, risk-scaled count-threshold ("M-of-N," here applied to reversal approval rather than the original transaction) is the standard default shape, not a GARBATI invention.
- **Regulatory-grade investigation standard for disputes, as a form of "approval."** Regulation E's error-resolution process functions as a risk-scaled approval gate in substance even though it is not phrased as "maker-checker": the institution must investigate and decide within 10 business days for most cases, or — if it cannot finish in time — give **provisional credit** within that same 10 days and take up to 45 total days, with a *maximum $50 withholding* allowed only if it has "a reasonable basis for believing an unauthorized transfer occurred." New-account, point-of-sale and foreign-initiated transactions get an extended 90-day investigation window instead of 45. [eCFR 12 CFR Part 1005](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/chapter-X/part-1005); [Canarie — Regulation E Error Resolution Timeframes](https://www.canarie.ai/blog/regulation-e-error-resolution-timeframes); [America's Credit Unions — Provisional Credit under Regulation E](https://www.americascreditunions.org/blogs/compliance/provisional-credit-under-regulation-e). This is a different "who signs off" mechanism than maker-checker (it is a *regulatory deadline with escalating remedy*, not a second human's signature), but it fills the same functional role of "more scrutiny before money moves for riskier/larger claims."
- **Card-network chargebacks already scale formally by category, not just amount**: Visa and Mastercard both route a dispute through one of four functional categories (fraud, authorization problem, processing error, consumer/cardholder dispute) with different evidentiary and timing requirements per category (see Q3/Q5) — i.e. the "approval" a chargeback needs is determined by *reason type* as much as by size. [Chargeflow — Visa Chargeback Reason Codes](https://www.chargeflow.io/blog/visa-chargeback-reason-codes); [Chargeflow — Mastercard Chargeback Reason Codes](https://www.chargeflow.io/blog/mastercard-chargeback-reason-codes).
- **Joint-account reversal consent — a genuine, unresolved gap.** I specifically searched for whether reversing a joint-account transaction requires the same multi-holder approval the *original* transaction needed, or is treated differently (any one holder, the original requester only, or an ops override), and **found no standard source that answers this directly.** The closest adjacent findings:
  - General joint-account practice distinguishes "joint ownership" (either owner can act alone, including closing the account) from "authorized signer" (acts only at the owner's discretion, does not own the funds) — but no source applies this distinction specifically to *who may request a reversal* of a transaction one of the joint owners made. [SoFi — What Is an Authorized Signer on a Bank Account?](https://www.sofi.com/learn/content/bank-authorized-signer/); [Wikipedia — Joint account](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint_account).
  - `research/joint-account-approval-patterns.md`'s own finding — that real banking mandate-*change* practice requires unanimous consent of all current holders, stricter than multisig's "current threshold" rule — is about changing the mandate/threshold itself, a different action from reversing one transaction, and should not be assumed to transfer directly.
  - One tangential finding on recovering a misdirected payment: a reversal "will not be approved without the consent of the party who received the payment" — this is about the *recipient's* consent on an EFT-style reversal claim, not about multi-holder consent on the *sender's* joint account, but it does establish that consent requirements in reversal contexts can attach to a different party than the one the original transaction's mandate governed. [general EFT reversal practice, secondary summary found via WebSearch — not independently corroborated by a second source]
  
  **This is a real open question for IdentityApi's design, not something this research resolves.** GARBATI's existing joint-account model (M-of-N mandate on the account) could plausibly apply the *same* threshold to a reversal request, a *lower* threshold (e.g. any one holder, since a reversal restores money rather than removes it), or route reversal requests through an ops/support override entirely regardless of the original mandate — no external standard was found that settles which of these is normal practice.

**What I could not verify:** (a) the joint-account-reversal-consent question above — this is the task's own Q2 sub-question and I could not find a direct answer; (b) any numeric amount-threshold a bank actually uses to decide when a reversal needs a second approver vs. a sole operator — sources describe the *existence* of tiered thresholds ("per Delegation of Authority") but not a concrete, generalizable number, because this is explicitly left to each institution's own internal policy.

## Q3: Standard reason codes

**Confidence: high** — both standard families (ISO 20022 and the card networks) were checked against multiple independent sources describing the same code sets.

**ISO 20022 / SEPA.** Reason codes travel inside the standard return/recall/reject messages and reuse the same underlying taxonomy across message types:

| Code | Meaning | Where used |
|---|---|---|
| `DUPL` | Duplicate payment/send | `camt.056` recall, `pacs.004` return |
| `TECH` | Technical problem at the sending institution | `camt.056` recall |
| `FRAD` | Fraudulent origin — initiated by an unauthorised party | `camt.056` recall |
| `CUST` | Customer requested (no claim of error/fraud) | Originator recall request; recall response decline reasons |
| `AM04` | Insufficient funds | Direct debit return; recall response |
| `AM05` | Duplication | Credit transfer / direct debit reject |
| `AC01` | Incorrect account number | Direct debit / credit transfer reject |
| `AC04` | Closed account number | Direct debit / credit transfer reject; recall response |
| `AC06` | Blocked account | Direct debit / credit transfer reject |
| `MD01` | No mandate | Direct debit reject |
| `MD06` | Refund request by end customer (within the 8-week SEPA chargeback-equivalent window) | Direct debit |
| `MD07` | End customer deceased | Direct debit / credit transfer reject |
| `RR01`–`RR04` | Missing information / regulatory reason | Reject, various |
| `NOAS` | No customer response | Recall response (`camt.029`) |
| `LEGL` | Legal decision | Recall response (`camt.029`) |

[SEPA/ISO 20022 reason codes — CPG glossary, full table read in full](https://www.cpg.de/en/glossary/overview-sepa-reason-codes/); cross-checked conceptually against [Analyst Engineering — ISO 20022 Reason Codes](https://www.analystengineering.com/articles/iso-20022-reason-codes) and [Medium — The Complete Map of ISO 20022](https://medium.com/@amitlokare/the-complete-map-of-iso-20022-8469a57b38c4), both of which describe the same reason-code role (travels alongside `pacs.002`/`pain.002` rejections, inside `pacs.004` returns, or in `camt.056` recalls) without contradicting the CPG table's content.

**Card networks.** Visa and Mastercard each sort disputes into four functional categories that map almost one-to-one onto each other and onto the ISO 20022 taxonomy above:

| Category | Visa (10.x–13.x) | Mastercard |
|---|---|---|
| Fraud | 10.x — cardholder/issuer claims the transaction was unauthorized | 4837, 4870, 4871, 4849 |
| Authorization problem | 11.x | 4808 |
| Processing error | 12.x — wrong amount, duplicate charge, etc. | 4834 ("Point-of-Interaction Error") |
| Consumer/cardholder dispute | 13.x — complaint about goods/services/billing | 4853 (plus 4850 for installment billing, 4854 for certain US domestic disputes) |

[Chargeflow — Visa Chargeback Reason Codes: Full 2026 Merchant List](https://www.chargeflow.io/blog/visa-chargeback-reason-codes); [Chargeflow — Mastercard Chargeback Reason Codes: 2026 List & Evidence](https://www.chargeflow.io/blog/mastercard-chargeback-reason-codes); cross-checked against [Sift — Mastercard Chargeback Reason Codes](https://sift.com/resources/trust-and-safety-university/mastercard-reason-codes/) and [Oscilar — Chargeback Reason Codes Explained](https://oscilar.com/blog/chargeback-reason-codes-explained), both independent of Chargeflow and describing the same four-category structure.

**The underlying categories are the same everywhere checked**: duplicate/technical processing error, fraud/unauthorized, customer dispute/complaint, insufficient funds, account-state problem (closed/blocked/deceased), and a residual "requested, no error claimed" bucket. This gives GARBATI an existing, well-tested vocabulary to reuse rather than invent — see the recommendation section below.

**What I could not verify:** I did not locate and read the full, official ISO 20022 "External Code Sets" primary-source spreadsheet published by ISO 20022's own registration authority (the CPG table above is a secondary compilation, consistent with but not identical to the primary registry); and I did not find a primary Visa/Mastercard rulebook PDF directly (the chargeback reason-code tables above come from payments-industry vendor sites — Chargeflow, Sift, Oscilar — which are consistent with each other across independent sources but are not Visa's or Mastercard's own published rulebooks, which are not freely public.

## Q4: Reconciliation when a reversal can't fully undo an external effect

**Confidence: medium** — the general accounting/operational pattern is well-documented; GARBATI-specific or Nigeria-specific application of it is not found and should not be assumed.

- **The core distinction the task asks about — "can claw this back" vs. "this is now a loss" — is a real, standard, named distinction**, not something GARBATI would be inventing: a **charge-off/write-off** is explicitly defined as "an accounting event where a lender... declares a debt uncollectible and writes it off the books... after recovery efforts have failed," and critically "this does not eliminate the debt — the borrower still owes the full amount — but it reflects a reduced expectation of recovery." A later successful collection on a written-off debt is recorded separately as a **recovery**. [Sardine — What Is Charge Off? Stop Fraud from Hiding in Credit Losses](https://www.sardine.ai/learn/charge-off); [Ramp — Bad Debt Recovery: A Guide for Business Owners](https://ramp.com/blog/bad-debt-recovery-journal-entry).
- **This switch is time-bound and policy-driven, not automatic or arbitrary**: regulatory guidance generally requires "the prompt write-off of financial assets when available information confirms the assets to be uncollectible," and gives concrete reference periods elsewhere in banking practice (e.g. a commonly-cited ~180-day delinquency trigger for unsecured consumer credit in US practice) — i.e. real institutions have an explicit decision point, not an indefinite "still trying" state. [Charge-off vs 1099-C guidance, cross-checked against general OCC/FDIC charge-off-policy framing](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/2022/02/can-lender-pursue-debt-collection-charge-off-1099-c-issuance/).
- **What happens operationally while the money is still being chased, before the write-off decision**: (a) a **lien/hold** on the disputed funds (seen concretely in Nigeria's own CBN framework — see Q5 — capped at two weeks); (b) **watch-listing** the recipient (CBN's BVN watch-list mechanism, per Q5); (c) a formal **collections process or legal/criminal referral** against the recipient, which several sources frame as a real deterrent — UK guidance on misdirected payments notes that knowingly keeping money received in error can be "dishonestly retaining a wrongful credit" under the Theft Act 1968, with civil recovery action also available [compare-the-market / lovemoney — Guides to Misdirected Payments](https://www.comparethemarket.com/current-accounts/content/misdirected-payments/); [lovemoney — Paid the wrong person?](https://www.lovemoney.com/news/91297/sent-money-to-the-wrong-account-get-money-back-after-misdirected-payment). The UK's own "misdirected payments code of best practice" expects banks to act within **2 working days** to start recovery and to usually conclude within **20 working days**, while explicitly disclaiming a guarantee of recovery — confirming that "we are trying to claw this back" is itself a bounded, time-limited phase, not an open-ended promise.
- **Fraud losses are a named, standardized category of operational risk, not an ad hoc write-off**: the Basel operational-risk framework's seven loss-event categories include "Internal Fraud" and "External Fraud" as two of the seven, classified by *who committed the act* rather than who lost the money — i.e. a bank's own regulatory reporting framework already has a slot for "this loss happened because of a fraud we couldn't recover," distinct from credit losses or processing-error losses. [Basel Committee operational risk event-type categories, cross-checked across multiple summaries](https://www.bis.org/publ/bcbs96.pdf). US banking regulators separately confirm that "banks experiencing fraud and fidelity losses may have future recoveries from insurance" — i.e. a fidelity bond or insurance policy is a standard, named mechanism for absorbing part of an otherwise-unrecoverable loss, distinct from the write-off itself.
- **Chargeback-specific accounting**: industry guidance on chargeback accounting treats a chargeback loss (principal plus the card network's chargeback fee) as its own line item distinct from an ordinary refund, because — unlike a cooperative refund — the merchant has no control over the timing or amount and must reconcile it against revenue after the fact. [Chargebacks911 — Chargeback Accounting: How it Works](https://chargebacks911.com/accounting-chargebacks/). This is the closest available real-world analogue to GARBATI's own "chain reversal fails entirely, nothing refunded" situation: a party absorbs a loss it did not choose the timing of, and must reconcile it as a distinct accounting event rather than pretend the original transaction simply didn't happen.

**What I could not verify:** no source was found describing *exactly* this scenario — an internal ledger's all-or-nothing reversal failing specifically because a chain's effects have already been consumed elsewhere — because that is a GARBATI-specific architectural choice (the all-or-nothing chain reversal, ARCHITECTURE.md decision #45/D-7), not a standard industry pattern with its own name. The write-off/charge-off, lien/watch-list, and Basel-fraud-category findings above are the closest real-world analogues, applied by inference, not a direct match. I also did not find any Nigeria-specific (CBN/NDIC) rule on *when* an unrecovered erroneous-transfer amount must be formally written off or reported as a loss — `research/nigerian-regulatory-requirements.md` does not cover this either, and a compliance officer should be asked specifically about this gap.

## Q5: Operational response-time expectations

**Confidence: high** — multiple independent, concrete, numeric sources, including a Nigeria-specific primary-adjacent source.

**The general pattern, confirmed across several independent rails: the bank's own error gets a short, fixed, unilateral window; a customer dispute gets a longer, investigation-bound window.**

- **Bank's own error — fast and fixed.** NACHA requires the ACH originator/ODFI to transmit its own Reversing Entry within **5 banking days** of the original Settlement Date — a short, hard deadline for the institution correcting its own mistake, with no customer claim or investigation step required. [NACHA — ACH Network Rules: Reversals and Enforcement](https://www.nacha.org/rules/reversals-and-enforcement).
- **Customer dispute — slower, bound to an investigation, often with interim relief.** US Regulation E gives the institution **10 business days** to investigate and decide, extendable to **45 calendar days total** if it provides **provisional credit** within the first 10 days (90 days for new accounts, POS, or foreign-initiated transfers); the institution must also notify the consumer within 2 business days of giving provisional credit. [eCFR 12 CFR Part 1005](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/chapter-X/part-1005); [Canarie — Regulation E Error Resolution Timeframes](https://www.canarie.ai/blog/regulation-e-error-resolution-timeframes); [America's Credit Unions — Provisional Credit under Regulation E](https://www.americascreditunions.org/blogs/compliance/provisional-credit-under-regulation-e). ACH's own receiver-side dispute window for an *unauthorized* entry is **60 days** from the date the consumer contacts their bank (return code R11) — twelve times longer than the 5-banking-day window the same rail gives the originator to fix its own mistake (return code R17, for a non-consumer, is only 2 days). [NACHA — ACH Network Rules: Reversals and Enforcement](https://www.nacha.org/rules/reversals-and-enforcement).
- **Card-network chargebacks confirm the same asymmetry, on even longer timelines.** Cardholders generally get **120 days** from the transaction date to file a dispute (Visa and Mastercard both; shorter — 75 or 90 days — for certain fraud/data-mismatch categories), while merchants must respond within **30 days per phase** (Visa) or **45 days** (Mastercard, US) once a dispute is filed. A small number of categories (Visa future-delivery disputes) extend to 540 days. [Chargebacks911 — Visa Chargeback Time Limits: The 2026 Guide](https://chargebacks911.com/visa-chargeback-time-limit/); [Chargebacks911 — Mastercard Chargeback Time Limits: The 2026 Guide](https://chargebacks911.com/chargeback-rules/chargeback-time-limits/mastercard-chargeback-time-limit/); [ClearSale — Chargeback time limits of major credit card issuers](https://www.clear.sale/blog/what-is-the-time-limit-on-chargebacks).
- **Nigeria (CBN) — concrete, close-to-primary numbers, cross-referencing but not duplicating `research/nigerian-regulatory-requirements.md`.** CBN's **Regulation on Instant (Inter-Bank) Electronic Funds Transfer Services** (issued 13 September 2018) and a later CBN circular revising electronic-channel refund timelines (effective 8 June 2020) set out a tiered schedule consistent with the general pattern above:
  - Failed **"on-us" ATM** transactions: reversed **instantly**; manual fallback within **24 hours** if the automatic reversal fails.
  - Failed **"not-on-us" ATM"** transactions: refunded within **48 hours**.
  - Disputed/failed **POS or web** transactions: resolved within **72 hours**.
  - **Instant EFT disputes** generally: concluded within **2 working days**; if the parties cannot agree, either may escalate to CBN's Banking and Payments System Department within one week of arbitration.
  - **Wrong transfer caused by bank error specifically**: the sending institution must write to the receiving institution within **14 working days** of the transaction, and the receiving institution must then reverse the funds within **1 business day** of receiving that request.
  - A **lien** placed on disputed funds pending resolution lasts **no more than 2 weeks**; internal-auditor mediation between the two institutions, where used, must also conclude within **2 weeks**.
  - If the receiving customer (beneficiary) does not voluntarily return a wrongful credit, the receiving institution must notify them of consequences — including being **watch-listed** across the banking industry and credit bureaux, and referral to law enforcement — within **24 hours**, and the watch-listing/reporting can follow **7 days** after notice without a refund.
  [Mondaq — Resolving Erroneous Electronic Transfers Without Court Orders: Examining the CBN Regulation on Instant (Inter-Bank) Electronic Funds Transfer Services, fetched and read via this research](https://www.mondaq.com/nigeria/financial-services/1647760/resolving-erroneous-electronic-transfers-without-court-orders-examining-the-central-bank-of-nigeria-regulation-on-instant-inter-bank-electronic-funds-transfer-services); the June 2020 revised-timelines circular itself is referenced via [Proshare — CBN Revises Timelines for Dispense Errors, Refund Complaints](https://proshare.co/articles/cbn-revises-timelines-for-dispense-errors-refund-complaints?menu=Regulators&classification=Read&category=CBN+Circulars++) and [ITEdgeNews — CBN revises timelines for dispense errors, refund complaints on electronic channels](https://www.itedgenews.africa/cbn-revises-timelines-for-dispense-errors-refund-complaints-on-electronic-channels/), **but I could not fetch either the primary CBN circular PDF nor the Proshare/ITEdgeNews pages directly** (both attempts returned errors) — the figures above for the on-us/not-on-us/POS/web tiers come from the search-engine-returned summaries of those pages, not text I read myself, and should be treated with the same caution `research/nigerian-regulatory-requirements.md` applies to its own secondary-source figures. The 14-working-day / 1-business-day / 2-week-lien figures, by contrast, come from the Mondaq article, which I did fetch and read directly, and which itself names and quotes the underlying 2018 CBN Regulation.
- **UK misdirected-payments code of best practice** (a different jurisdiction, cited for pattern comparison only, not as a Nigerian or Sharia rule): banks are expected to act within **2 working days** to start recovery of a misdirected payment and usually conclude within **20 working days**, without a guarantee of recovery. [Comparethemarket — Guide to Misdirected Payments](https://www.comparethemarket.com/current-accounts/content/misdirected-payments/).

**What I could not verify:** I could not fetch CBN's own circular PDF directly (same 403-style access problem `research/nigerian-regulatory-requirements.md` already documented repeatedly for cbn.gov.ng), so the specific on-us/not-on-us/POS/web tier figures above rest on secondary summaries, not a primary document I read myself — a compliance officer should confirm these against the live CBN circular before IdentityApi's design relies on them. I also did not find any operational-response-time standard specific to Sharia-compliant/non-interest institutions as distinct from conventional ones — nothing found suggests a different timeline applies, but nothing confirms that either.

## Recommended default shape for GARBATI

**This is a recommendation for the owner to accept, change, or reject. It is not a decision, and reversal authority is explicitly one of AGENTS.md's human-only gates (it changes money rules and authorization).** It deliberately does not touch the already-fixed ledger mechanics (ARCHITECTURE.md D-7, decision #45) — it is about the layer above that mechanics.

1. **Who may initiate, split by the same "whose error/claim is it" axis found everywhere in Q1:**
   - **Bank/system error** (duplicate chain posted, wrong amount due to a software bug, a known orchestrator/ledger failure already identified by ops): any authenticated **internal operator** may initiate, without requiring the customer to ask — matching the "bank's own error, unilateral and fast" pattern (NACHA 5-banking-day self-reversal; CBN's instant on-us ATM reversal). This path should still be logged and auditable, but does not need customer-facing dispute machinery.
   - **Customer dispute of someone else's action** (fraud claim, "I didn't authorize this," "goods never arrived," billing error): **customer-initiated** through a channel, which opens an investigation case rather than an immediate reversal — matching the Reg E / chargeback pattern of claim-then-investigate-then-decide, not instant execution.
   - **Customer simply wants a push payment pulled back, no error claimed**: treat as its own, narrower category (reusing ISO 20022's `CUST` concept — see item 3 below), likely requiring the recipient's cooperation/consent before it can be executed non-coercively, consistent with the one (unverified-by-a-second-source) finding in Q2 that reversal without the receiving party's consent is generally not simply granted.
   - **Failed/bounced settlement detected automatically by the system itself** (the ledger or orchestrator detects its own chain never completed): fully automated, no human initiator at all, matching CBN's and NACHA's auto-reversal patterns for this category.

2. **Approval scaling with risk, reusing GARBATI's existing maker-checker/M-of-N vocabulary rather than inventing a new one:**
   - A **low-risk reversal** (small amount, bank's-own-error category, reversing a chain that posted only seconds/minutes ago and has not yet had any downstream effect) could reasonably be single-operator-initiated with standard audit logging, similar to a "maker" role acting within its own posting limit.
   - A **higher-risk reversal** (larger amount, customer-dispute category, or any reversal where the ledger might fail the all-or-nothing chain-reversal check because funds have moved on) should require a second, independent approver — a "checker" distinct from whoever requested or investigated the case — mirroring the core-banking "dual control for back-value/reversal entries" pattern found in Q2, and consistent with the maker-checker pattern GARBATI already uses elsewhere in this design.
   - **The specific amount threshold that separates these two tiers is a GARBATI-specific policy choice this research cannot supply** — every source found leaves the exact number to the institution's own Delegation of Authority; the owner (or a future ProductApi-level configuration) should set it, informed by GARBATI's actual transaction-size distribution once there is data.
   - **Joint-account reversal consent is the one sub-question this research could not answer from any standard source (see Q2).** Recommend treating it as its own explicit open design question rather than silently inheriting the original transaction's M-of-N threshold: consider whether a reversal (which restores money to the account) should require a *lower* bar than the original debit did (e.g. any one holder, since it is reducing risk rather than creating it) — but flag this as a recommendation to debate, not a resolved answer, since no external precedent was found either way.

3. **A starter reason-code set, reusing ISO 20022's and the card networks' already-converged categories (Q3) instead of inventing GARBATI-specific names:**
   - `DUPL` — duplicate posting (system/bank error)
   - `TECH` — technical/processing error (system/bank error)
   - `FRAD` — fraudulent origin (customer dispute, investigation required)
   - `CUST` — customer requested, no error claimed (requires recipient's cooperation per item 1 above)
   - `UNAUTH` *(GARBATI-specific, modeled on the Reg E / chargeback "unauthorized transaction" concept — no single ISO 20022 code maps directly to this, since ISO 20022's codes are bank-to-bank message codes, not consumer-claim codes)* — customer disputes a transaction as never authorized by them
   - `DISPUTE` *(GARBATI-specific, modeled on card-network 13.x/4853 "consumer dispute" categories)* — customer disputes an authorized transaction's outcome (e.g. counterparty didn't deliver what was promised, for a financing/merchant-style product later in GARBATI's roadmap)
   - Recommend **not** inventing a GARBATI-specific code for "insufficient funds" or "closed/blocked account" reversal triggers — these already exist as ledger-level failure reasons (ARCHITECTURE.md's existing error taxonomy, 422 business-rule failure) and do not need a separate reversal-reason code layered on top.

4. **Reconciliation/write-off process shape, modeled on the Q4 findings (lien/watch-list/collections while recoverable, formal write-off once not):**
   - **Recoverable phase** (immediately after a chain-reversal fails because an external effect can't be undone): place an internal flag/hold equivalent to a "lien" on the record, start an internal collections-style process against the recipient (demand notice, then escalate), and set an explicit, short, bounded window for this phase — recommend something in the range of CBN's own 1–2 week lien/mediation windows (Q5) rather than leaving it open-ended, since an open-ended "still trying" state is exactly what the write-off literature (Q4) warns against.
   - **Loss-to-absorb phase** (the bounded recovery window above expires without recovery): record a formal write-off as its own auditable event, distinct from the original failed reversal — not a silent "give up," but a deliberate, logged decision with its own reason and amount, consistent with the charge-off accounting pattern in Q4. Whether GARBATI needs its own loss-absorption/reserve mechanism (an equivalent to a fidelity-bond or insurance recovery line) is a separate product/finance decision this research does not resolve, but it is the standard mechanism real institutions use to fund losses of this kind (Q4).
   - **Regulatory reporting**: flag, do not resolve, that CBN/NDIC may have its own reporting requirement for unrecovered amounts of this kind — `research/nigerian-regulatory-requirements.md` does not cover this and neither does this brief; a compliance officer must be asked specifically.

5. **Operational response-time targets, modeled on the clear asymmetry found in Q5:**
   - **Bank/system-error reversals**: aim for the "CBN on-us ATM" / "NACHA 5-banking-day" end of the spectrum — same-day to a few business days, since no customer investigation is needed.
   - **Customer-dispute reversals**: aim for something in the Reg E / CBN instant-EFT range already found — an initial response within roughly **2 business days** (matching CBN's own instant-EFT-dispute standard) with a definite outer bound (CBN's instant-EFT framework uses roughly a working week before escalation; Reg E uses 10/45/90 days as a ceiling) — rather than leaving dispute resolution time unbounded.
   - Recommend an explicit internal SLA distinguishing these two tiers be written into IdentityApi's or the orchestrator's eventual reversal design, rather than a single blanket response-time target for every reversal reason code.

## What this does not settle

- **Does not decide anything.** This is research to inform the owner's and coordinator's eventual design of IdentityApi's reversal authority (T-015) — it is not that design, and it does not resolve any of the items the IdentityApi design draft's own Section 9 item 8, or `Garbati.IdentityApi/docs/DESIGN.md`'s current Section 8, already named as open ("who may initiate a reversal, required reason codes/approvals, the expected operational response time, and how reconciliation handles external effects that a ledger reversal cannot undo by itself").
- **Does not re-litigate the ledger mechanics.** ARCHITECTURE.md's D-7 (all-or-nothing chain reversal, no remediation path yet, 10-leg maximum, `Principal`/`Fee`/`Tax` purpose codes only) stays exactly as decided; nothing here proposes changing it.
- **Not a Sharia ruling.** Two relevant items were found and must be flagged, not treated as settled: (a) the Islamic-finance concepts of **Qard Hasan** (an interest-free benevolent loan) and **Ibra** (a voluntary rebate/write-off of debt, described in the literature as a recommended, charitable act, particularly toward an insolvent debtor) may bear on how GARBATI frames or names a formal write-off of an unrecoverable reversal loss, especially if the unrecoverable amount is owed by a customer in genuine hardship rather than a fraud actor [ResearchGate — The Principles of Qard Hasan in Debts and Receivables](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/392668903_THE_PRINCIPLES_OF_QARD_HASAN_IN_DEBTS_AND_RECEIVABLES_OF_STRENGTHENING_THE_UMMAH_ECONOMY_AND_SHARIA_FINTECH_REGULATION); [FundingSouq — Procrastinating Debtor in Islamic Finance](https://fundingsouq.com/ae/en/blog/procrastinating-debtor-in-islamic-finance-a-shariah-based-guide-of-debt-in-islam/); (b) whether a GARBATI write-off/collections process toward a recipient who has not returned a wrongful credit is itself something that must distinguish "innocent recipient who already spent money in good faith" from "a recipient acting in bad faith" is exactly the kind of question a qualified Sharia scholar, not this research, must answer — this brief only flags that the distinction exists in the literature, it does not resolve how GARBATI should apply it.
- **Not a Nigerian regulatory ruling.** The CBN figures in Q5 (instant-EFT dispute windows, lien duration, watch-listing timeline) come from a mix of a directly-read secondary legal-analysis article (Mondaq) and search-engine summaries of pages this research could not fetch directly — consistent with the access problems `research/nigerian-regulatory-requirements.md` already documented repeatedly for cbn.gov.ng. **A qualified compliance officer must confirm every CBN figure in this brief against the live, current circular before IdentityApi's reversal design relies on it** — exactly the same caveat `research/nigerian-regulatory-requirements.md` already applies to its own findings, extended here to this brief's additional CBN-specific figures (the 2018 Regulation on Instant EFT Services, and the 2020 revised electronic-channel refund timelines).
- **Does not answer the joint-account-reversal-consent question** named in the task's own Q2 — no standard source was found addressing whether reversing a joint-account transaction needs the same multi-holder consent the original transaction needed. This is reported as a genuine, unresolved gap, not glossed over.
- **Does not replace the owner's own judgment.** The amount thresholds, exact reason-code names, and exact response-time numbers in the "Recommended default shape" section above are this researcher's synthesis of the nearest real-world patterns found — none of them are a cited, settled standard specific to GARBATI's situation, and the owner may reasonably choose different numbers or a different shape entirely.
