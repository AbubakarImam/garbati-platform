Output informs the design questions in T-009 (ProductApi). **This document does not decide anything.** It is background reading assembled from public sources, not a fatwa or a compliance ruling. Every claim below must be confirmed by a qualified Islamic finance scholar (ideally the Sharia advisor GARBATI eventually appoints) and, separately, by whoever is responsible for Nigerian regulatory compliance, before any of it is treated as a product rule.

## Summary in plain words

Conventional interest (riba) — any fixed, guaranteed extra amount charged on a loan just for the loan existing over time — is not allowed. Microfinance institutions get around this by not making pure cash loans with interest. Instead they either:
- sell something to the customer at a markup, paid in instalments, with the markup fixed at the start and never increasing because of time or default (**Murabaha**, cost-plus sale);
- go into a business partnership with the customer and share real profit and real loss in agreed proportions (**Musharaka**/**Mudarabah**); or
- lend money with no profit at all, expecting back only the exact amount lent (**Qard Hasan**, "goodly loan").

For savings accounts, the no-interest version is usually either a safekeeping arrangement where the bank promises to return exactly what was deposited (Wadiah/Qard-based current or savings account), or a profit-sharing investment account (Mudarabah) where the customer's return is not guaranteed and depends on the bank's actual profit.

On late payment: a bank cannot charge extra as "interest" for lateness, and cannot increase the amount owed on the contract. What is widely used instead — and what multiple sources confirm Nigeria's regulator contemplates for non-interest banks — is a late-payment charge that is paid into a charity fund the bank does not keep or benefit from, and even that is only meant to apply to a customer who can pay but is deliberately refusing to, not to someone who genuinely cannot pay.

On fees generally: a fee is on firmer ground when it reflects an actual, real, calculable cost of providing the service (a flat administrative or processing cost), and is on much weaker ground when it is a percentage of the amount or time outstanding, because that starts to look like disguised interest.

AAOIFI (a Bahrain-based standard-setting body for Islamic finance) publishes named standards for most of this — Murabaha, Musharaka, Mudarabah, Qard, and specifically a standard on a debtor who delays payment. These are the standards practitioners cite most often, including in Nigeria's own regulatory language. I could not read the full official AAOIFI standard texts (they sit behind the AAOIFI member/purchase wall); what follows is built from secondary summaries and should be checked against the primary text where it matters.

Confidence overall: **medium**. The general shape (no riba, use sale/partnership/benevolent-loan structures, late fees to charity, fee must equal real cost) is consistently repeated across independent sources including Nigeria's own regulatory commentary. The fine print (exact conditions, how "actual cost" is calculated, whether any compensation beyond charity is ever allowed) varies between sources and schools of thought, and needs a scholar's sign-off before it becomes a rule in code.

## Question 1: What is riba, and what structures replace it for savings and short-term financing?

**Riba** is any predetermined, guaranteed increase charged on a loan or debt purely for the passage of time, regardless of the rate — this is the core prohibition cited across sources, grounded in Qur'an 2:275-279 ([halalwallet.us, "What is Riba?"](https://www.halalwallet.us/what-is-riba); [halalwallet.us, "Is a Business Loan Haram?"](https://www.halalwallet.us/blog/is-a-business-loan-haram-2026)). A pure cash loan that must be repaid with anything extra, on account of time elapsed, is the paradigm case of what is prohibited.

Structures used instead, for the kinds of products GARBATI is likely to build first:

- **Murabaha (cost-plus sale).** The institution buys something the customer wants (goods, an asset, working-capital inventory) and resells it to the customer at a disclosed cost plus an agreed, fixed markup, usually paid in instalments. The markup is fixed at contract signing and does not change because of time or default — it is a sale price, not interest ([Wikipedia, "Murabaha"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murabaha); [cbonds.com glossary, "Murabaha"](https://cbonds.com/glossary/murabaha/); AAOIFI Shariah Standard No. 8, cited at [aaoifi.com](https://aaoifi.com/shariah-standards-3/?lang=en)). This is the instrument most often named for short-term, asset-linked financing in both general literature and Nigeria-specific sources ([Leonine, "Islamic Microfinance in Nigeria"](https://leonine.com.ng/islamic-microfinance-in-nigeria/); [kudicompass.com](https://kudicompass.com/non-interest-banking-nigeria-guide/)).

- **Musharaka (partnership/joint venture).** Both parties contribute capital; profit is shared by an agreed ratio, loss is shared in proportion to capital contributed — there is no guaranteed return to either side (AAOIFI Shariah Standard No. 12, "Sharikah (Musharakah) and Modern Corporations," [aaoifi.com](https://aaoifi.com/shariah-standards-3/?lang=en); [Leonine](https://leonine.com.ng/islamic-microfinance-in-nigeria/)).

- **Mudarabah (profit-sharing, one party capital / one party effort).** One party (e.g. the depositor, or the bank) provides capital, the other provides labour/expertise; profit is split by agreed ratio, loss is borne by the capital provider alone (unless the working partner was negligent) (AAOIFI Shariah Standard No. 13 and No. 40 on profit distribution, [aaoifi.com](https://aaoifi.com/shariah-standards-3/?lang=en); [Leonine](https://leonine.com.ng/islamic-microfinance-in-nigeria/)). This is commonly used for savings/investment accounts where the customer's return is not fixed and depends on real bank profit.

- **Qard Hasan (benevolent/goodly loan).** A loan with zero profit of any kind — the borrower repays exactly the amount borrowed, nothing more. Widely used in Islamic microfinance for small, social or emergency lending, and sometimes as the underlying structure for current/savings accounts (the depositor is treated as having given the bank a Qard Hasan, repayable on demand) ([Wikipedia, "Qard al-Hasan"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qard_al-Hasan); AAOIFI Shariah Standard No. 19, "Loan (Qard)"; [kudicompass.com](https://kudicompass.com/non-interest-banking-nigeria-guide/)).

- **Wadiah (safekeeping) for savings/current accounts.** A deposit held by the bank, which guarantees the nominal amount back on demand; the depositor does not share in profit or loss. Used as an alternative deposit-account basis alongside Qard Hasan or Mudarabah ([ResearchGate, "Saving and Current Account in the Context of Shari'ah Contract"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/286433231_Saving_and_Current_Account_in_the_Context_of_Shari'ah_Contract)).

For Nigeria specifically, CBN's regulatory language for non-interest institutions describes the sector as "based on the concept of profit and loss sharing rather than charging of interest," and names Murabaha, Musharaka, Mudarabah, Ijarah (leasing) and Istisna (manufacturing/construction finance) as the standard product set ([Leonine](https://leonine.com.ng/islamic-microfinance-in-nigeria/); [kudicompass.com](https://kudicompass.com/non-interest-banking-nigeria-guide/); [Legal500, "Islamic Finance In Nigeria: 2024 Year In Review"](https://www.legal500.com/intelligence/nigeria/finance-and-banking/islamic-finance-in-nigeria-2024-year-in-review-and-2025-outlook)).

**Confidence: medium-high** for the general definitions and names; **low** for the precise legal conditions each contract must satisfy to actually be valid (e.g. Murabaha requires genuine ownership/risk transfer of the asset before resale — a detail I could not verify in depth and that is frequently where "Islamic" products are criticized as not being truly Sharia-compliant in practice).

## Question 2: How are late payments and defaults handled? Is a late fee permitted, and does it go to charity?

This directly addresses the open question flagged in `ARCHITECTURE.md` Section 9 ("Sharia rules... late-fee-to-charity").

**What I found supports the "late-fee-to-charity" framing, with an important condition attached.**

- The relevant AAOIFI standard is **Shariah Standard No. 3**, titled (per [aaoifi.com](https://aaoifi.com/shariah-standards-3/?lang=en)) **"Procrastinating Debtor"** — also referred to in secondary sources as "Default in Payment by a Debtor" ([islamicfinance.com summary](https://www.islamicfinance.com/2015/06/aaoifi-standard-number-three-default-payment-debtor/)).
- According to that summary, the standard distinguishes a **solvent debtor who is deliberately delaying payment** ("procrastinating") from a debtor who is **genuinely unable to pay** (insolvent/in hardship). The late-charge mechanism described below is aimed only at the first case; a genuinely struggling customer is treated differently (the sources consistently reference the Qur'anic instruction to grant relief or extension to a debtor in real difficulty, though I did not find a precise operational rule for what "relief" must look like).
- It is **not permitted** to add the amount or percentage as financial compensation that the institution keeps, and a court cannot impose that either, per the islamicfinance.com summary of Standard 3.
- The one structure repeatedly described as acceptable: the contract can include an **upfront undertaking by the customer** that, if they default while solvent and deliberately delaying, they will pay a charitable amount — and that amount must be spent on charitable causes **under the supervision of the institution's own Sharia board**, not retained as income by the institution ([islamicfinance.com](https://www.islamicfinance.com/2015/06/aaoifi-standard-number-three-default-payment-debtor/); corroborated by [SeekersGuidance, "What Is the Ruling of Penalty Fees Given to Charity?"](https://seekersguidance.org/answers/shafii-fiqh/what-is-the-ruling-of-penalty-fees-given-to-charity/), which states "AAOIFI Standards (2017) explicitly state that late fees are also permissible if the fees are paid to a charity," while also noting this is not unanimous among scholars and recommends checking with a local mufti).
- For Nigeria specifically: secondary sources describing the non-interest banking regime are consistent that "late payment penalties in non-interest banking are not interest income for the bank. They are redirected to charity" ([kudicompass.com, "Non interest banking: Islamic Banking in Nigeria"](https://kudicompass.com/non-interest-banking-nigeria-guide/)), citing CBN's 2011 "Guidelines for the Regulation and Supervision of Institutions Offering Non-Interest Financial Services." I located the primary CBN guideline documents (the 2011 circular at [cbn.gov.ng](https://www.cbn.gov.ng/OUT/2011/CIRCULARS/FPR/NON-INTEREST%20BANKING%20GUIDELINES%20JUNE%2020%202011.PDF) and a 2016 draft specific to non-interest microfinance banks at [cbn.gov.ng/out/2016/fprd/nimfb.pdf](https://www.cbn.gov.ng/out/2016/fprd/nimfb.pdf)) but **could not fetch or read the primary PDF text directly** (request blocked/forbidden) — this claim rests on secondary sources describing the CBN rule, not my own reading of the CBN document. A 2016 news report confirms CBN issued these guidelines for non-interest MFBs but the article I could access did not itself spell out the late-fee mechanism ([ThisDay, "CBN Unveils Guidelines for Non-interest MFBs"](https://www.thisdaylive.com/2016/05/12/cbn-unveils-guidelines-for-non-interest-mfbs/)).
- One source presents a **harder line**: a WebFetch summary of the islamicfinance.com page (same source as above) also stated the standard "provides no allowance for ta'widh (actual loss compensation) or gharamah (penalties)" beyond the charity mechanism, and that the debtor bears the creditor's legal recovery expenses but not opportunity-cost or currency-devaluation compensation. Another independent search result described the penalty as serving "as a means of deterrence... as well as to compensate for actual loss the bank suffers," which reads as a slightly different (looser) characterization. These two characterizations are not fully consistent with each other on whether "actual loss" compensation (ta'widh) is separately allowed outside the charity mechanism — **this specific point needs a scholar to resolve**, since it affects whether GARBATI could ever recover a real, documented cost of collection versus only ever routing money to charity.

**Does this confirm or correct the ARCHITECTURE.md Section 9 note?** It **confirms the direction** — a late-payment charge going to a charity fund the institution does not profit from is the standard mechanism described across every source I found, including Nigeria-specific commentary — but it adds conditions ARCHITECTURE.md does not currently capture: (a) it should only trigger for a customer who is solvent and deliberately delaying, not one in genuine hardship; (b) the charity disbursement needs governance (a Sharia board or equivalent oversight) confirming the money actually reaches a charitable cause and never reaches the institution's revenue; (c) whether any separate, real-cost recovery (collection/legal costs) can be charged on top of or instead of the charity amount is unsettled between my sources and needs a scholar's ruling.

**Confidence: medium.** The headline rule (late fee to charity, not to the bank) is well corroborated. The edge conditions (solvent-vs-hardship distinction, governance of the charity fund, whether cost-recovery is separately allowed) are not fully settled across my sources.

## Question 3: What charges are generally prohibited vs permitted?

**Generally prohibited** (consistent across sources):
- Any charge that is effectively a disguised interest payment — i.e., calculated as a percentage of the outstanding amount and the time it has been outstanding ([ResearchGate, "Determination of Actual Cost Framework in Islamic Financial Product"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/312946226_Determination_of_Actual_Cost_Framework_in_Islamic_Financial_Product); [Dar Al Iftaa Jordan, "Charging the Loanee with Administrative Expenses is Permissible with Conditions"](https://aliftaa.jo/research-fatwa-english/3073/Charging-the-Loanee-with-Administrative-Expenses-is-Permissible-with-Conditions), which states loan costs "shouldn't be tied to the amount of the loan or its period as a percentage... they must be tantamount to the true cost, without any increase").
- Increasing the amount owed on a contract (e.g. a Murabaha sale price) because of late payment or because the term is extended/rescheduled — described as "undisputedly forbidden" by the sources I found ([search summary citing Islamic finance fiqh commentary](https://seekersguidance.org/answers/halal-and-haram/is-a-loan-that-charges-a-1-fee-for-lending-permissible/)).
- A fee charged purely for the institution's lending of money as money (a pure interest-equivalent fee on a Qard Hasan) — one fatwa source states plainly "imposing a late payment fee is impermissible" in the context of a Qard loan specifically, as distinct from the charity-undertaking mechanism in Murabaha-type sale contracts described in Q2 above. (Note: this appears to conflict, on its face, with the charity-late-fee mechanism discussed in Q2 — the likely reconciliation is that the charity-undertaking is treated by AAOIFI as not being a "fee" in the prohibited sense because the institution never keeps it, but I could not confirm this reconciliation from a primary source — **flagging for scholar review**.)

**Generally considered more defensible / closer to permitted:**
- A **flat fee that reflects a genuine, calculable, actual cost** of providing a service (processing, administration, documentation) — repeatedly described as acceptable as long as it does not exceed real cost ([Dar Al Iftaa Jordan](https://aliftaa.jo/research-fatwa-english/3073/Charging-the-Loanee-with-Administrative-Expenses-is-Permissible-with-Conditions); [SeekersGuidance, "Is a Loan That Charges a 1% Fee for Lending Permissible?"](https://seekersguidance.org/answers/halal-and-haram/is-a-loan-that-charges-a-1-fee-for-lending-permissible/)).
- A **fixed markup agreed at contract signing** in a genuine sale contract (Murabaha) — this is a sale price, not a fee on a loan, and is the mainstream structure, provided it does not change afterward.
- Percentage-based fees are described by at least one source as potentially acceptable **if structured as a service/agency fee for a real, variable cost of service** (e.g. a payment-gateway-style fee, structured as ji'ala — payment for a defined task) rather than as a charge for the use of money over time ([SeekersGuidance, "Are Percentage-Based Payment Gateway Fees Permissible in Islam?"](https://seekersguidance.org/answers/shafii-fiqh/are-percentage-based-payment-gateway-fees-permissible-in-islam/)). This is a narrower and more contested exception than the flat-fee case, and the dividing line between "legitimate variable service cost" and "disguised interest" is exactly the kind of judgment call that needs a scholar, not a researcher, to make for any specific GARBATI fee.

**Confidence: medium.** The core distinction — real cost recovery is defensible, time/amount-based percentage charges on a debt are not — is consistent and intuitive, and matches the general riba logic in Q1. The exact boundary cases (percentage-of-amount service fees, administrative fee caps, how "actual cost" must be documented or audited) are genuinely contested among scholars in the sources I found, not just unverified by me.

## Question 4: Are there existing published standards worth citing directly?

Yes — **AAOIFI (Accounting and Auditing Organization for Islamic Financial Institutions)**, based in Bahrain, is the standard-setting body named across almost every source consulted, including Nigeria-specific regulatory commentary. Per [aaoifi.com's Shariah Standards index](https://aaoifi.com/shariah-standards-3/?lang=en), the standards most relevant to GARBATI's first products are:

| Standard | Title | Relevance |
|---|---|---|
| SS 3 | Procrastinating Debtor | Late payment / default handling (Q2) |
| SS 8 | Murabahah | Cost-plus financing structure (Q1) |
| SS 9 | Ijarah and Ijarah Muntahia Bittamleek | Leasing/lease-to-own, likely a later product |
| SS 12 | Sharikah (Musharakah) and Modern Corporations | Partnership financing (Q1) |
| SS 13 | Mudarabah | Profit-sharing investment/savings accounts (Q1) |
| SS 19 | Loan (Qard) | Qard Hasan structure (Q1) |
| SS 40 | Distribution of Profit in Mudarabah-Based Investment Accounts | Savings-account profit distribution mechanics |

**I could not read the full primary text of any of these standards.** AAOIFI's site lists titles and numbers but the full standard text sits behind a members'/purchase access wall that I was not able to get past with the tools available to me. Everything stated about standard content above (what SS 3 actually says about procrastinating debtors, etc.) comes from **secondary summaries**, principally [islamicfinance.com's write-up of Standard 3](https://www.islamicfinance.com/2015/06/aaoifi-standard-number-three-default-payment-debtor/) and search-engine-generated summaries of other pages — not from AAOIFI's own published wording. Anyone using these standards to write actual product rules should obtain the primary AAOIFI text (or a licensed practitioner's direct citation of it), not rely on this brief.

For Nigeria, the relevant **regulatory** (not Sharia-scholarly) documents are CBN's:
- "Guidelines for the Regulation and Supervision of Institutions Offering Non-Interest Financial Services" (2011) — [cbn.gov.ng](https://www.cbn.gov.ng/OUT/2011/CIRCULARS/FPR/NON-INTEREST%20BANKING%20GUIDELINES%20JUNE%2020%202011.PDF)
- "Guidelines for the Regulation and Supervision of Non-Interest (Islamic) Microfinance Banks in Nigeria" (draft referenced 2016/2017) — [cbn.gov.ng/out/2016/fprd/nimfb.pdf](https://www.cbn.gov.ng/out/2016/fprd/nimfb.pdf)
- A 2024 capital-requirement update for non-interest banks (minimum capital for a National non-interest banking licence raised to NGN 20bn, Regional to NGN 10bn; existing institutions must comply by March 2026) — [Legal500, "Islamic Finance In Nigeria: 2024 Year In Review and 2025 Outlook"](https://www.legal500.com/intelligence/nigeria/finance-and-banking/islamic-finance-in-nigeria-2024-year-in-review-and-2025-outlook)

**I was blocked (HTTP 403) when trying to fetch the primary CBN PDF documents directly**, so the specific wording of CBN's own late-fee/charity rule is something I have not personally read — only secondary descriptions of it (see Q2). Whoever is responsible for Nigerian regulatory compliance should obtain and read these CBN documents directly, since CBN licensing/supervision requirements are a legal obligation, not just a Sharia preference, for any Nigerian non-interest microfinance bank.

**Confidence: medium** that these are the right standards to point to; **low** on the precise content of each standard, since I only read secondary summaries, not primary text.

## Question 5: What does this research NOT settle?

Everything in this document is a starting point for a design conversation, not a ruling. Specifically unresolved, and requiring a qualified Islamic finance scholar or Sharia board (and, separately, Nigerian regulatory/compliance sign-off):

1. **Whether GARBATI's actual product mechanics will count as genuine Murabaha/Musharaka/Mudarabah/Qard Hasan**, or whether they risk being "Islamic in form, conventional in substance" — a well-documented criticism of some real-world Islamic finance products. This requires a scholar reviewing the actual contract flow, not a general description.
2. **The exact conditions for a valid Murabaha** (e.g., genuine ownership and risk transfer of the underlying asset before resale to the customer) — I found this mentioned as a requirement but did not verify the specifics.
3. **The solvent-vs-hardship distinction for late payment.** My sources agree a genuinely struggling customer should be treated differently from one deliberately delaying, but none gave an operational definition of "genuine hardship" or what relief (grace period, rescheduling without increase, write-off) is required.
4. **Whether any cost-recovery charge (e.g. real collection or legal cost) is allowed on top of, or instead of, the charity-bound late fee.** My sources genuinely disagreed with each other on this point (see Q2); it needs a scholar to resolve, not more of my searching.
5. **Governance of the charity fund** — who verifies the money collected as a late fee actually reaches a charitable cause and never becomes institutional revenue; what counts as an eligible charitable cause; whether this needs a dedicated ledger account (ARCHITECTURE.md Section 9 already flags a possible `Charity` leg purpose code — this research supports that idea existing, but not its governance detail).
6. **The dividing line for percentage-based fees** — when a percentage fee is a legitimate service/agency charge (ji'ala) versus disguised interest. At least one source treats this as contested among scholars, not a settled rule.
7. **The precise content of AAOIFI Standards 3, 8, 12, 13, 19, 40** — I only read secondary summaries; the standards' primary text was not accessible to me.
8. **The precise content and current wording of CBN's non-interest (Islamic) microfinance bank guidelines** — I could not fetch the primary CBN PDFs; only secondary descriptions of them.
9. **Savings-account specifics** — whether GARBATI's first savings product should be Wadiah-based (capital-guaranteed, no profit share) or Mudarabah-based (profit-sharing, not guaranteed), and what minimum profit-sharing ratio disclosure or reserve requirements would apply, is not something I found a settled, citable answer for.
10. **Anything school-of-thought-specific** (Hanafi vs Shafi'i vs Maliki vs Hanbali, or specific national fiqh council rulings) — several sources I found disagreed with each other or noted scholarly disagreement explicitly (e.g. the SeekersGuidance piece on penalty fees). I have not resolved which school or council GARBATI's own Sharia board will follow, nor should I — that is a decision for the owner and the appointed scholar, not for this research.
