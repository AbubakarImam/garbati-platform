# Lien / auto-sweep patterns for unrecoverable reversals (T-021)

**Task:** T-021. **Status:** Research only — no code, no design decision. This extends `research/reversal-authority-patterns.md` (T-019), specifically Q4 and Q5, on one narrow mechanical question T-019 did not settle: when a reversal's money is genuinely unrecoverable *right now*, do real systems place a **standing lien that automatically captures future incoming funds and auto-debits the moment they arrive, with no new manual approval**, or is the "decide once: recoverable vs. write-off" pattern T-019 already found the real default? This does not repeat T-019's findings on who initiates a reversal, approval scaling, reason codes or general response-time windows — read T-019 first; it is assumed knowledge here.

---

## Plain-language summary (read this first)

A few terms, defined once, specifically for this question:

- **Lien / hold (as used here):** an internal flag a financial institution puts on an account that blocks some amount of money from being withdrawn or used, because the institution has a claim on it. The critical mechanical question this brief tests: **does a lien, by default, only block money already sitting in the account at the moment it is placed, or does it also reach forward in time and grab money that arrives later?** The evidence below says: by default, almost everywhere checked, **a lien is a snapshot — it only holds what is there right now.** Capturing future money too is a different, more specific thing (see "continuing" mechanisms below), not something an ordinary lien does automatically.
- **Right of setoff / right of offset:** a bank's legal right to use money sitting in a customer's deposit account to pay off a *separate, matured debt* that same customer owes the bank (classically, an unpaid loan). This is a different legal tool from recovering a credit the bank mistakenly *sent in the first place* — see Q4 below, which the task specifically asked to be kept separate.
- **Auto-sweep (as the task uses it):** an account-draining or debt-recovery mechanism that, without a new human decision at the moment, automatically pulls money out the instant a balance check passes — as opposed to a system that merely *notices* the balance is now sufficient and puts the decision in front of a human.
- **Write-off (reused from T-019):** the formal accounting act of declaring a debt uncollectible and taking it off the books as a loss. It does not forgive the debt; a later "recovery" is still possible and recorded separately.

**Short answer to the five questions:** (1) A mechanism that matches the owner's exact description — an indefinite, fully-automatic, third-party-erroneous-credit-recovery standing lien — was **not found as a single named standard banking practice.** What exists instead is a family of related but narrower things: point-in-time "snapshot" liens (the default everywhere checked, including what little is knowable about CBN's own lien), a bounded same-transaction retry mechanism on the ACH rail (NACHA's R01/R09 re-presentment, capped at two extra tries), and a regulator-sanctioned automatic-deduction-from-future-loads mechanism on prepaid/GPR cards — but that last one is the issuer recovering **its own** extended credit from **its own** customer under an advance signed contract clause, which is a materially different situation from recovering someone else's erroneous windfall. (2) Whether the actual debit at the moment of sufficiency is **fully automatic** or **detected-then-queued-for-a-human** could not be answered for the owner's exact scenario from any source found — this is reported as a genuine, unresolved gap, matching the task's own framing of it as "the crux." (3) CBN's existing 2-week lien figure (already found in T-019) appears, by every general pattern checked, to describe a point-in-time hold on "the erroneously transferred amount" — a fixed, already-identified sum — not a standing claim against indefinite future inflows, but this is an inference by analogy to the general pattern found elsewhere, **not an independent confirmation from CBN's own text**, which remains inaccessible (same `cbn.gov.ng` access problem T-019 already documented). (4) Recovering an erroneous credit rests on a **different and broader legal basis** than the bank's right of setoff for a customer's own pre-existing debt: US sources converge on the common-law doctrine of **unjust enrichment / the law of mistake and restitution**, which exists independently of any contract term, and which UCC Article 4A's own erroneous-funds-transfer provision (§4A-303) explicitly defers to rather than inventing its own mechanism — but real banks *also*, separately and additionally, write an explicit advance-authorization clause into their deposit account agreements covering exactly this case, so in practice the basis is doubled up: contract **and** independent restitution right. (5) No source — T-019's existing citations or new searches run for this task — answers whether CBN's framework includes a mechanism for automatically capturing *future* incoming funds; this remains an open gap, not newly resolved here.

## Q1: Does the standing-lien-with-auto-sweep mechanism exist, under what name, and is it the standard pattern or a sub-case?

**Confidence: medium** on the general taxonomy below (several independent, convergent sources across different legal/banking domains); **low** that a mechanism matching the owner's exact description exists as a single, named, standard practice — it was not found.

- **The default, everywhere checked, is a point-in-time "snapshot" lien/hold that does *not* reach into the future.** Core-banking "lien marking" (the actual operational feature behind freezing part of a balance, e.g. in Finacle/TCS BaNCS-class systems) places a hold "only on the money that is in the account at the time the lien is given to the bank... deposits made after the lien has been filed... are not subject to the lien." [Outlook Money — Lien Marked On Your Bank Account? Here's How To Remove It](https://www.outlookmoney.com/banking/lien-marked-on-your-bank-account-heres-how-to-remove-it); [QuickLend — What Is Lien Marking and Why Does It Matter?](https://www.quicklend.in/learning/lien-marking-why-it-matters). The same rule holds in US debt-collection law for bank-account garnishments and IRS bank levies: "a bank levy only reaches money that was in the account on the day the levy hit. Deposits made after that date are safe, unless [a] new levy [is issued]." [Alper Law — Can You Open a New Bank Account After a Levy or Garnishment?](https://www.alperlaw.com/asset-protection/exempt-bank-account/levied-bank-account-new-account/); [IRS — IRM 5.11.4 Bank Levies](https://www.irs.gov/irm/part5/irm_05-011-004).
- **A standing claim that *does* reach into the future exists, but only as a distinct, specially-authorized category, not a default property of liens.** US wage garnishment/levy law treats **wages** differently from a **bank account balance** for exactly this reason: a continuing wage garnishment "needs to be applied only once and will be applicable to future wages until... released... so as future wages are earned, no additional levy action is necessary" — explicitly contrasted against a bank-account levy, which reaches only the balance present at service and requires a brand-new levy to reach anything deposited afterward. [Sam Brotman — IRS Wage Garnishments: What You Need to Know](https://sambrotman.com/the-complete-guide-to-irs-collections/irs-wage-garnishments/); some US states also allow a specifically-named "continuing garnishment" order for the same reason. [Idaho Legislature — Title 11, Chapter 7, Garnishments](https://legislature.idaho.gov/wp-content/uploads/statutesrules/idstat/Title11/T11CH7.pdf). The pattern: **reaching forward into future money is always its own, separately-authorized legal construct (a continuing order), never an automatic side-effect of placing an ordinary lien.**
- **The closest real analogue to "auto-capture money when it arrives, no new approval" is a bounded, same-transaction retry, not a standing lien.** NACHA's ACH rules let an originator **reinitiate** (resubmit) a debit entry that was returned for **R01 (Insufficient Funds)** or **R09 (Uncollected Funds)**, up to **two additional attempts**, each within a defined window, and — critically — **this reinitiation does not require a new customer authorization**, unlike reinitiating after an *unauthorized*-transaction return code (R05/R07/R10/R29), which does. [Ramp — ACH Return Codes Explained: R01 to R85](https://ramp.com/blog/ach-return-codes); [Payliance — Nacha ACH Rule Highlights](https://payliance.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/Nacha-ACH-Rule-Highlights-2.pdf). This is the clearest standard-rail precedent for "no fresh consent needed to try again once funds might be there" — but it is a **capped, originator-decided retry of the same transaction**, not an open-ended, bank-system-triggered standing claim.
- **The clearest case of a genuinely automatic deduction from future money, with regulatory sanction, is prepaid/GPR card overdraft-style "credit feature" recovery — but it recovers the issuer's *own* extended credit, not a third party's erroneous windfall.** US federal rulemaking on prepaid accounts permits an issuer to "deduct all or part of the cardholder's credit [feature] debt automatically from the prepaid account... no more frequently than once per month, pursuant to a signed, written authorization by the cardholder," with a minimum **21-day** repayment grace period before the feature may even engage. [CFPB — Final Rule: Prepaid Accounts under EFTA (Regulation E) and TILA (Regulation Z)](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/20161005_cfpb_Final_Rule_Prepaid_Accounts.pdf); [Federal Register — same rule](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2016/11/22/2016-24503/prepaid-accounts-under-the-electronic-fund-transfer-act-regulation-e-and-the-truth-in-lending-act); general Reg E framework at [eCFR 12 CFR Part 1005](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/chapter-X/part-1005). This is a materially different situation from GARBATI's: the issuer is collecting a debt **it voluntarily extended as credit to its own customer**, under **advance signed consent obtained for that exact purpose**, within **explicit regulatory frequency/grace-period limits** — not clawing back an erroneous credit that arrived in someone else's account by mistake.
- **Right of setoff is real, legally self-executing, but is a different tool aimed at a different kind of debt, and is not described anywhere found as a continuously-running automated sweep.** It is called a "self-help remedy" that "exists independent of statutory authority and may be exercised without court supervision," usable "without getting a court order, giving the customer prior notice, or getting the customer's permission." [University of Oklahoma Law Review — Banker's Right of Setoff: Banker Beware](https://digitalcommons.law.ou.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2180&context=olr); general descriptions corroborate this, while also noting at least one account of a bank that "seldom exercised" the right even where eligible — implying a deliberate, selective act by the institution, not a passive, always-on sweep [found via search-engine summaries of multiple US consumer-legal explainer sites, including Nolo, Thompson Law Office, and uslawexplained.com — I could not fetch any of these three directly (403 errors on each); the underlying claims are consistent across all three but are reported here as secondary-source paraphrase, not text I read myself]. Setoff also specifically requires the customer's debt to the bank to be **matured** — i.e. it targets a pre-existing, separate debt (a loan), the exact category the task's Q4 asks to be kept distinct from erroneous-credit recovery (see Q4 below).

**Conclusion on Q1:** the task's exact proposed mechanism — an indefinite, fully-automatic, specifically-erroneous-third-party-credit-recovery standing lien — is **not** a single named standard banking practice found anywhere in this research. The actually-documented, real-world pattern is the opposite default (snapshot liens that do not reach forward), with narrower, specially-bounded exceptions (ACH's capped retry; prepaid cards' advance-consented, frequency-capped auto-deduction of the issuer's own credit; and specially-ordered continuing garnishments for a different kind of debt). **This supports T-019's existing finding (Q4) that the recoverable-vs-write-off switch is "a deliberate, time-bound decision, not automatic"** as the better-evidenced default, not a one-time opinion of that research alone — this brief independently reaches the same conclusion from a different set of sources.

**What I could not verify:** no primary core-banking vendor technical specification (Finacle, TCS BaNCS, or any other) was read directly — the "lien marking" description above comes from Indian retail-banking consumer-explainer sites, not vendor documentation, though it is internally consistent with the independently-sourced US garnishment/levy-law pattern. I also could not independently confirm the Nolo/Thompson Law/uslawexplained setoff claims by reading the primary pages myself (all three returned 403 errors to direct fetch); they are reported as cross-corroborating but unread-by-me secondary sources.

## Q2: Is the actual debit automatic, or does a human still approve it at the moment funds arrive?

**Confidence: low — this is reported as an unresolved gap, not an answer.** This is the exact question the task names as "the crux," and no source found addresses it directly for the owner's specific scenario (recovering a third party's unrecoverable, erroneous reversal amount from an account that later gets funded).

What the adjacent patterns suggest, without resolving it:

- **ACH reinitiation (R01/R09)** is a decision the **originator** makes to resubmit — described in NACHA materials as something the originator "may" do, within a bounded window, up to two extra times. This is "automatic" in the sense that no *new customer authorization* is needed, but the *re-attempt itself* is a deliberate act by the originator's own process (which could itself be automated software, or could involve a human collections decision) — it is not the bank's core system passively watching the account and triggering a debit the instant a balance check passes. [Ramp — ACH Return Codes Explained](https://ramp.com/blog/ach-return-codes).
- **Right of setoff** is described as something the bank "can exercise... without getting... the customer's permission," which answers "no new *customer* consent is needed" but does not answer whether the bank's *own internal* process requires a human officer's decision to pull the trigger at that moment — if anything, the "some banks seldom exercise it" observation above (Q1) suggests setoff is typically a deliberate, selective institutional act, not a silently-running automated sweep, though this is a thin, single, unread-by-me secondary-source data point, not a confirmed rule.
- **Prepaid-card credit-feature auto-deduction** is the one case where the regulatory language itself uses the word "automatically" for the deduction from a subsequent load — this is the strongest evidence found anywhere in this research that a fully-automatic, no-new-approval-at-the-moment debit upon funds arriving is a real, regulator-sanctioned banking mechanism. But, as noted in Q1, it recovers the issuer's own extended credit under advance written consent obtained specifically for that purpose, which is not the same legal or operational situation as recovering an erroneous reversal amount from an account that never agreed in advance to anything of the kind.
- **No source was found describing the third-party-erroneous-credit case specifically** — i.e., nothing confirms or denies whether, in real institutions, a recipient's account getting funded again after an unrecovered reversal triggers (a) a fully silent, system-executed debit, or (b) a notification/queue that a human in collections or ops must then act on before money actually moves.

**What I could not verify:** this entire question, for the specific scenario the task asks about, remains unanswered by any source found. It is plausible that this operational detail is simply not publicly documented anywhere, because it sits inside banks' internal collections/core-banking operational procedures rather than in published regulation, consumer-facing disclosures, or industry-standard rail rules — none of which were designed to answer "what exactly happens, mechanically, inside the bank's system" at this level of detail. A banking-operations or core-banking-systems specialist, not public-web research, is likely the only way to settle this definitively.

- **General pattern (not CBN-specific):** every snapshot-lien/levy/garnishment source found above (Q1) treats the lien as applying to a **fixed, already-identified amount**, not an evolving claim — consistent with T-019's own Q4 finding that the recoverable-to-write-off switch is itself "a deliberate, time-bound decision, not automatic," and with the UK misdirected-payments code of best practice T-019 already found (2 working days to start recovery, usually concluding within 20 working days, with no guarantee of recovery) — a bounded window, after which the process (not an auto-sweep) determines the outcome. [research/reversal-authority-patterns.md Q4, re-confirmed here]; [Comparethemarket — Guide to Misdirected Payments, already cited in T-019](https://www.comparethemarket.com/current-accounts/content/misdirected-payments/).

[Mondaq — Resolving Erroneous Electronic Transfers Without Court Orders](https://www.mondaq.com/nigeria/financial-services/1647760/resolving-erroneous-electronic-transfers-without-court-orders-examining-the-central-bank-of-nigeria-regulation-on-instant-inter-bank-electronic-funds-transfer-services). The phrase "on the erroneously transferred amount" (a specific, fixed sum, already quantified) reads, by analogy to the general snapshot-lien pattern found everywhere else in this research, as a point-in-time hold — **but the article itself does not say this explicitly**, and does not describe what (if anything) happens to money the recipient's account receives *after* the lien is placed but before the 2 weeks run out. The general banking-literature pattern (Q1/Q4 above) is that expiry of a bounded recovery window is a deliberate decision point (renew, escalate, or write off), not an automatic flip to either state — but this is inference by the general pattern, not a CBN-specific confirmation.

## Q4: What is the legal/regulatory basis for debiting an account later without fresh consent, specifically for recovering an erroneous/unauthorized credit (distinct from setoff for a pre-existing debt)?

**Confidence: medium-high** for the US legal picture, which converges across several independent sources; **not assessed** for Nigerian law specifically — flagged as a gap, consistent with T-019's own repeated caveat about Nigerian-specific legal confirmation.

- **The primary, contract-independent basis: the common-law doctrine of unjust enrichment / the law of mistake and restitution.** "Financial institutions have the authority to reverse erroneous transactions automatically once the error is discovered, often without the account holder's consent," and courts generally "will allow the paying party to recover the money" under unjust enrichment — described as applying "no matter if the situation occurs accidentally or in error," with no requirement of fraud or deliberate wrongdoing on the recipient's part. [HCR Law — A business has paid money by mistake: can it get it back?](https://www.hcrlaw.com/news-and-insights/a-business-has-paid-money-by-mistake-can-it-get-it-back/); [MFG Solicitors — Unjust Enrichment](https://www.mfgsolicitors.com/site/blog/mfg-blog/unjust-enrichment); [LegalClarity — If a Company Pays You by Mistake, Can They Take It Back?](https://legalclarity.org/if-a-company-pays-you-money-by-mistake-can-they-take-it-back/). This right exists **independently of any contract term** — it is a general equitable/common-law right, not something the bank must have pre-negotiated with the customer.
- **US commercial law's own funds-transfer statute defers to that same general doctrine, rather than inventing a separate mechanism.** UCC Article 4A §4A-303, governing erroneous execution of a wire-transfer payment order (overpayment, underpayment, or wrong beneficiary), states in each case that the bank "is entitled to recover from the beneficiary... the [erroneous] payment received **to the extent allowed by the law governing mistake and restitution**" — i.e. Article 4A does not create its own standing-lien or auto-sweep right; it explicitly routes back to the general unjust-enrichment doctrine above. [North Carolina codification of UCC 4A-303](https://www.ncleg.gov/EnactedLegislation/Statutes/PDF/BySection/Chapter_25/GS_25-4A-303.pdf); [Cornell LII — UCC Article 4A](https://www.law.cornell.edu/ucc/4a/article4a). This is the clearest direct evidence that recovering an erroneous credit and a bank's ordinary right of setoff are treated as **legally distinct tools** in US commercial law, exactly the distinction the task's Q4 asked to be kept separate: setoff requires a **matured, pre-existing, separate debt**; erroneous-credit recovery instead rests on mistake/restitution law, which has no such "separate matured debt" requirement.
- **In practice, banks additionally — not instead — build an explicit advance-consent clause into the account-opening contract covering this exact case.** Real deposit-account-agreement boilerplate was found stating: "The Bank reserves the right, and the Customer does hereby authorize the Bank to debit the Customer's Account with respect to any funds erroneously credited to the Customer's Account due to a computer system error, technical error or malfunction, human error, clearing system error or any other reason, without any liability and/or claim arising against [the Bank]." [LawInsider — Erroneous Transactions clause, fetched and read directly](https://lawinsider.com/clause/erroneous-transactions). Separately, US ACH-credit-specific contract language found elsewhere confirms the same pattern for ACH credits specifically: a credit from an ACH entry is "provisional until final payment is received," and if final payment is not received, the bank "may reverse the credit... or you will reimburse them," and "if the bank receives satisfactory documentation that a credit entry was made in error, it may debit your account for the amount erroneously credited" [found via search-engine summary across several US bank deposit-agreement PDFs, including Commerce Bank's — https://www.commercebank.com/personal/bank/deposit-agreement — not independently read in full by me]. **So the basis in practice is doubled up**: an independent common-law restitution right (which would exist even without a contract term) **plus** an explicit advance-authorization clause banks write into account terms anyway, almost certainly to avoid any dispute about consent later and to simplify internal operational execution.
- **The CFPB's own consumer guidance treats this as settled and simple:** asked "A deposit was credited to my account by mistake. Can the bank/credit union take it back?", the CFPB's answer is simply "Yes" — with no further detail on mechanism, timing, or legal theory on that specific page. [CFPB — Ask CFPB, fetched and read directly](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/a-deposit-was-credited-to-my-account-by-mistake-can-the-bankcredit-union-take-it-back-en-1015/).

**What I could not verify:** everything above is US (and, for the account-agreement boilerplate, likely also drawing on general international commercial banking practice) legal doctrine and contract practice — **no Nigerian-specific legal source was found or checked for whether Nigerian contract law, any Nigerian unjust-enrichment/restitution doctrine, and CBN's own regulation together provide the same dual (contract + independent-restitution-right) basis.** This must not be read as Nigerian legal advice; a qualified Nigerian lawyer or compliance officer must confirm the equivalent basis (if any) under Nigerian law before GARBATI relies on any of the above for a Nigerian customer relationship. This is the same category of caveat T-019 already applies throughout to anything not confirmed against a Nigerian primary source.

## How this changes (or doesn't) the recommended shape

**This is a recommendation for the owner to accept, change, or reject. It is not a decision**, and (per AGENTS.md) any change to reversal/write-off mechanics is a human-only-gate item regardless.

**Against T-019's existing recommendation:** T-019's Q4 "recoverable phase → loss-to-absorb phase" shape already assumed the lien/hold during the recoverable phase is a **bounded, time-limited flag** (explicitly recommending "something in the range of CBN's own 1–2 week lien/mediation windows... rather than leaving it open-ended," and warning that "an open-ended 'still trying' state is exactly what the write-off literature warns against"). **This research does not contradict that recommendation — it independently reinforces it from a different angle.** Everywhere checked for this task (core-banking lien-marking, US garnishment/levy law, UCC 4A's deference to general restitution law, ACH's capped reinitiation, prepaid cards' frequency- and grace-period-capped auto-deduction), the real-world default is **bounded and point-in-time**, not open-ended and forward-reaching. An indefinite standing claim that silently watches an account forever, waiting for enough money to appear, is not what real systems do by default anywhere found in this research — the owner's belief, as stated, describes the *exception* (continuing garnishment orders; advance-consented, regulator-capped credit-feature recovery), not the norm, and even those exceptions are bounded by an explicit order, contract clause, or regulatory cap, never open-ended.

**Against `Garbati.IdentityApi/docs/DESIGN.md` Section 8.6:** the current design (decision #34) deliberately keeps `ReversalOutcomes` a **thin, two-state audit record** (`Completed`/`WriteOff`) and explicitly pushes "the actual recovery process — demand notices, watch-listing, legal/criminal referral, when the recoverable phase formally ends and becomes a write-off" entirely outside IdentityApi (8.6, 8.8), undesigned and out of scope. **This research does not find a real-world precedent that compels adding a third state** (e.g. `LienPending`) with a built-in auto-sweep trigger, because:
1. The genuinely-automatic, no-new-approval cases found (prepaid-card credit-feature deduction) are the issuer recovering **its own** extended credit from **its own** customer, under **advance signed consent obtained for that exact purpose** — a different legal and operational shape than GARBATI recovering an unrecoverable reversal amount, where no comparable advance consent currently exists in the design.
2. The bounded-retry case found (ACH R01/R09 reinitiation) is a **capped, originator-decided re-attempt of the same specific transaction** (max 2 extra tries, defined window) — closer in spirit to "try the chain reversal again a couple of times within a short window" than to "maintain an indefinite standing claim."
3. Q2's crux — whether any real system executes this kind of debit with zero human step at the moment of sufficiency, for *this* scenario specifically — remains unanswered by any source found, so there is no real-world precedent to point to either confirming or ruling out full automation if the owner wanted to build it.

**So: the existing two-state design is not shown to be inadequate by this research.** If the owner nonetheless wants GARBATI to build an active, re-triggerable recovery mechanism (closer to ACH's bounded reinitiation than to an indefinite lien), the research here suggests it should, if built: (a) be explicitly bounded in time, consistent with T-019's own recommendation and CBN's 1–2 week figure, not open-ended; (b) be built and framed as **GARBATI's own deliberate policy choice**, not as "what real systems normally do," since the evidence points the other way; and (c) explicitly decide — because no external precedent settles it — whether the eventual debit, if the bounded window is extended into an active retry mechanism, executes with zero new human approval or instead queues for one, since that is exactly the unresolved crux of Q2 and the owner's own stated design question.

**Net recommendation:** keep `ReversalOutcomes`'s existing `Completed`/`WriteOff` binary as currently designed; do not add a third state on the strength of this research alone, since no real-world standard was found requiring it. If the owner wants an auto-sweep capability anyway (as a deliberate GARBATI product choice, not because "real banks do this"), treat it as a new, explicitly-scoped feature with its own bounded time window and its own explicit automatic-vs-queued decision — informed by, but not compelled by, anything found here.
