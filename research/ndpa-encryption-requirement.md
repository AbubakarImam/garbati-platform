# Does Nigerian law actually require encrypting BVN/NIN at rest?

Researcher note: this is background research, not legal advice and not a compliance sign-off. **A qualified compliance officer must confirm every reading below before it is used to decide how GARBATI stores (or doesn't store) BVN/NIN.** Where I quote a primary document I read the full text myself (not a summary of it); where I rely on a secondary source I say so explicitly.

## Plain-language summary — the direct answer to the owner's question

**No. The primary Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 ("NDPA") text does not contain a specific, binding rule that says "you must encrypt BVN/NIN at rest."** What it contains is a general, risk-based duty — "appropriate technical and organisational measures" — and encryption is named only once, as one example out of seven, in a sentence that says data controllers "may" use it, not that they must. This is a real, meaningful difference from what a prior brief in this repo (`research/oss-core-banking-readiness-gaps.md`, item G3) reported from secondary sources: that summary said NDPA "explicitly includes encryption at rest and in transit" as a requirement. Having now read the primary Act text directly, that characterization overstates what the Act says — it rounds a "may include" example up into a mandate. The correction matters for G3's design decision, so I'm flagging it plainly here rather than softening it.

That said, three things sit alongside this "no specific mandate" finding and are worth weighing, not ignoring:

1. **One genuinely specific, binding encryption rule does exist in Nigerian law relating to NIN — but it doesn't apply to GARBATI.** The National Identity Management Commission Act 2007, Section 21(2)(b), requires that NIMC's own Multipurpose Identity Card "record the prescribed part of it in an encrypted form." That is a real, specific, primary-text-verified encryption mandate — but it binds NIMC as the card issuer, about what NIMC puts on its own card. It does not regulate what a bank or fintech does when a customer types their NIN into an onboarding form and the bank stores that number in its own database. I found no equivalent provision anywhere binding third parties who collect and store NIN.
2. **BVN/NIN do not clearly fall inside the NDPA's own defined "sensitive personal data" category**, which is a closed list (genetic/biometric data, race/ethnicity, religion, health, sex life, political opinion, trade union membership, plus whatever the Commission separately prescribes) that does not name identification or financial numbers. BVN/NIN are unambiguously "personal data" (the Act's personal-data definition explicitly lists "an identification number" as an example identifier) — but "personal data" generally and "sensitive personal data" specifically carry different (lighter vs. heavier) obligations under the Act, and BVN/NIN sit in the lighter category unless the Nigeria Data Protection Commission ("NDPC") has separately prescribed them as sensitive. I could not find evidence that it has.
3. **NDPC's own current implementing instrument (the General Application and Implementation Directive, "GAID", 2025) treats encryption as something it expects a mature data controller to have considered and be able to self-report on** — its compliance-audit-return template explicitly asks whether an organisation "relies on data pseudonymisation, anonymisation and encryption methods to reduce exposure of personal data," and the absence of these is one of the facts an organisation can be marked down for during NDPC's annual review. That's real regulatory pressure, but it is still not phrased as "you must encrypt X" — it's phrased as a factor NDPC will look at when judging whether your overall security posture was "appropriate."

## Q1: Does the NDPA name encryption specifically as required, or only a general "appropriate measures" standard?

**Confidence: high.** I read the full primary text of the Nigeria Data Protection Act, 2023 (Act No. 37, 2022 No. 37 in the Federal Republic of Nigeria Official Gazette No. 119, 1 July 2023) directly, including Part VII "Data Security" in full.

The binding obligation is Section 39(1):

> "A data controller and data processor shall implement appropriate technical and organisational measures to ensure the security, integrity and confidentiality of personal data in its possession or under its control, including protections against accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, misuse, alteration, unauthorised disclosure, or access, taking into account — (a) the amount and sensitivity of the personal data ; (b) the nature, degree and likelihood of harm to a data subject that could result from the loss, disclosure, or other misuse of the personal data ; (c) the extent of the processing ; (d) the period of data retention ; and (e) the availability and cost of any technologies, tools, or other measures to be implemented relative to the size of the data controller or data processor."

That is the mandate. It is a risk-based, principles standard ("appropriate measures"), not a named-technology requirement. It does, however, give GARBATI real, usable factors to weigh: BVN/NIN's amount and sensitivity, the likely harm from a breach (identity theft, fraud), and GARBATI's own size/cost constraints as a small team — all legitimate inputs into deciding what "appropriate" means for this specific system.

Encryption is named — but only in Section 39(2), which lists *illustrative, optional* measures a controller "may" adopt to satisfy 39(1):

> "Measures implemented under subsection (1) may include — (a) pseudonymisation or other methods of de-identification of personal data ; (b) encryption of personal data ; (c) processes to ensure security, integrity, confidentiality, availability and resilience of processing systems and services ; (d) processes to restore availability of and access to personal data in a timely manner, in the event of a physical or technical incident ; (e) periodic assessments of risks to processing systems and services... ; (f) regular testing, assessing, and evaluation of the effectiveness of the measures implemented... ; and (g) regular updating of the measures and introduction of new measures to address shortcomings..."

Encryption is one of seven listed options, syntactically and legally on the same footing as the other six (pseudonymisation, resilience processes, disaster-recovery processes, risk assessments, testing, and updating). Nothing in the Act singles encryption out as mandatory while leaving the others optional. The operative verb for the whole subsection is "may include" — not "shall include" or "must include."

One nuance worth flagging honestly rather than hiding: NDPC's own current implementing directive (GAID 2025, Article 52, Definitions) states — about its own use of the word "may" *within the GAID* — that '"may" when used in the context of an obligation in this GAID, indicates that there exist accountability safeguards for regulatory actions for data controllers or data processors to take into consideration. It does not imply that the obligation is merely advisory.' This is NDPC signalling that it does not want "may"-worded provisions treated as throwaway options when it enforces. That definitional note is explicitly scoped to the GAID's own text, not to Section 39(2) of the Act (which is a different, primary-legislation-level document, not the GAID) — so it doesn't automatically reinterpret the statute. But it does tell you something about the regulator's general posture, and a compliance officer should not assume "may" in any NDPC-adjacent document is safely ignorable.

**Bottom line for Q1:** the binding legal duty is the general "appropriate technical and organisational measures" standard; encryption is named as one non-exclusive example of how to satisfy it, not as an independent requirement in itself.

## Q2: Is "sensitive personal data" a defined category under the Act, and does BVN/NIN fall into it?

**Confidence: high on the definition itself; medium-low on whether BVN/NIN is covered**, because the answer depends partly on whether NDPC has issued a regulation I haven't found.

Section 65 (Interpretation) defines "sensitive personal data" as personal data relating to an individual's:

> "(a) genetic and biometric data, for the purpose of uniquely identifying a natural person, (b) race or ethnic origin, (c) religious or similar beliefs, such as those reflecting conscience or philosophy, (d) health status, (e) sex life, (f) political opinions or affiliations, (g) trade union memberships, or (h) other information prescribed by the Commission, as sensitive personal data under section 30(2)..."

This is a closed, named list plus a catch-all for whatever NDPC separately prescribes. BVN and NIN — plain numeric identifiers — are not named anywhere in it. By contrast, the Act's general "personal data" definition (also Section 65) explicitly includes "an identification number" as a textbook example of an identifier: "any information relating to an individual, who can be identified or is identifiable, directly or indirectly, by reference to an identifier such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier or one or more factors specific to the physical, physiological, genetic, psychological, cultural, social, or economic identity of that individual." So BVN/NIN are squarely "personal data" — just not, on the Act's own wording, automatically "sensitive personal data."

This distinction matters practically: Section 30 imposes extra, consent-gated conditions specifically on processing "sensitive personal data" (consent, or one of several listed narrow exceptions like vital interest, legal claims, public interest with specific safeguards). If BVN/NIN are not "sensitive personal data" by the Act's own definition, those extra Section 30 gates do not automatically apply to them — only the general Section 24/39 obligations do.

Section 30(2) gives NDPC the power to prescribe further categories of sensitive personal data by regulation. I reviewed NDPC's current, operative implementing instrument — the General Application and Implementation Directive (GAID) 2025, including all of its 52 articles and 10 schedules — and found no provision that formally adds BVN, NIN, or "financial data" generally to the sensitive-personal-data list under this power. The closest the GAID gets is Article 19(5), about cookie consent, which exempts "necessary cookies which do not process sensitive data, financial data or any data stored privately by a data subject" from requiring an opt-in tick-box — language that treats "sensitive data" and "financial data" as two separate, parallel categories in that one specific cookie-consent context, not a formal designation of financial data as "sensitive personal data" under Section 30. I could not find a clearer or more general NDPC prescription.

**What I could not verify here:** whether NDPC has issued some other regulation, guidance notice, or directive (separate from the GAID) that specifically designates BVN, NIN, or "financial identifiers" as sensitive personal data under Section 30(2)/65(h). I searched for this and did not find one, but the absence of a hit in my search is not proof none exists.

## Q3: Does NDPC have published guidance, codes of practice, or enforcement history specifically naming encryption-at-rest as expected/required for identity numbers like BVN/NIN?

**Confidence: medium**, based on reading NDPC's full current implementing directive and researching its public enforcement record.

NDPC superseded the older Nigeria Data Protection Regulation (NDPR) 2019 and its 2020 Implementation Framework when it issued the GAID in 2025 (GAID Article 3(3): "Upon the issuance of the GAID, the Commission shall cease to apply the Nigeria Data Protection Regulation (NDPR) 2019 as a legal instrument for regulating data privacy and protection"). So the GAID, not the older NDPR materials, is the current operative guidance — and this is worth flagging because the older, now-superseded NDPR Implementation Framework (per secondary sources, since it has been withdrawn I did not re-verify its primary text) reportedly referenced ISO/IEC 27001 and "employing data encryption technologies" more directly than the GAID does.

In the current GAID, the clearest reference is Article 29(2)(g), part of the required "Schedule for Monitoring, Evaluation and Maintenance of Data Security System": a data controller or processor "shall have schedules for monitoring, evaluation and maintenance of data security systems," covering "applicable technical and organisational measures including but not limited to: (a) Training; (b) Certifications; (c) Updates of software; (d) Vulnerability tests of databases; (e) Assessment of hardware for repairs or replacements; (f) Authentication checks; (g) Encryption reviews; and (h) Quality Assurance..." This requires a controller to periodically *review* its encryption posture as part of an ongoing security-management schedule — it does not say encryption itself must exist, only that whatever security measures exist (which could in principle be none) must be subject to a documented review schedule that happens to list "Encryption reviews" as a line item.

The GAID's Schedule 2 (the annual NDP Act Compliance Audit Returns template every registered data controller must file) is more revealing of NDPC's expectations in practice. It asks organisations to self-report, among many other facts: "The organi[s]ation relies on data pseudonymisation, anonymisation and encryption methods to reduce exposure of personal data" versus "The organisation has no[t] implemented data pseudonymisation, anonymisation or encryption methods to reduce exposure of personal data" — presented as one of many optional self-assessment facts, not a pass/fail gate. The same schedule's data-security rating section (2(D)) grades an organisation on a scale from "Poor" to "Industry Grade," with the higher tiers explicitly referencing adherence to ISO 27000 series, NIST SP 800-series, NIST CSF, CIS Critical Security Controls, COBIT, or HITRUST — again descriptive benchmarks for self/DPCO-audit scoring, not a minimum legal floor stated as "you must reach at least tier X."

On enforcement history specifically: NDPC has become visibly more active. Public reporting (2025–2026) shows NDPC fined Fidelity Bank ₦555.8 million for processing personal data without lawful basis and informed consent, and fined MultiChoice Nigeria ₦766.2 million for unlawful cross-border data transfers and disproportionate data practices. NDPC has also issued compliance notices to 795 financial institutions as part of a broader sector sweep, and describes its own enforcement posture as moving through phases — "warnings and education (2019–2023), investigations and fines (2024–2025)," and "full enforcement mode (2026 onward)." **I did not find any NDPC enforcement action whose stated basis was specifically "failure to encrypt data at rest" or an equivalent encryption-specific security failing**, for a financial institution or otherwise. This is useful evidence of how strictly the encryption point specifically has been enforced so far (not very visibly, based on what I found) — but as the task brief correctly anticipated, that is evidence about enforcement practice, not evidence that unencrypted storage is legally compliant. NDPC's own stated move into "full enforcement mode" from 2026 onward means the absence of a precedent to date does not predict the absence of one going forward.

## Q4: Does CBN's BVN-specific framework impose a security/encryption requirement separate from and more specific than NDPA's general obligations?

**Confidence: low — this remains genuinely unverified, the same gap the prior brief (`research/nigerian-regulatory-requirements.md`, Q2) already flagged.** I made a renewed, different-angle attempt specifically for this task: I located and directly fetched the CBN "Revised Regulatory Framework for Bank Verification Number (BVN) Operations and Watch-List for the Nigerian Banking Industry" (2021) via a mirrored copy, and tried extracting its text with two different tools. Both attempts returned only image-scan content with no usable, readable text layer — the same failure mode the prior brief hit directly on cbn.gov.ng. I was not able to confirm or rule out whether this framework contains its own encryption or security-specific clause.

What secondary sources (same ones cited in the prior brief, not independently re-verified here) describe is tiered BVN-database access restriction — CBN-licensed financial institutions and certain tiers mediated through NIBSS — which is an access-control rule, not a confirmed encryption-at-rest rule. Nothing in what I found (primary or secondary) specifically addresses encryption of BVN data once an institution has lawfully obtained and stored it.

## Q5: Does CBN have a circular specifically about NIN handling, or has it adopted/cross-referenced NIMC's own data-protection rules for NIN?

**Confidence: high on what the NIMC Act itself says (I read it in full); low on whether CBN has cross-referenced it (not found, not ruled out).**

I read the full primary text of the National Identity Management Commission Act, 2007 (Act No. 23). It does contain one genuinely specific, binding encryption requirement — but it is narrowly scoped to NIMC's own identity card, not to third parties who later collect a customer's NIN:

> Section 21(2): "A Multipurpose Identity Card issued to any registered person shall — (a) record only the prescribed information or data ; (b) record the prescribed part of it **in an encrypted form** ; (c) be only for the prescribed period ; and (d) remain the property of the Federal Republic of Nigeria."

This is a real, named, primary-source-confirmed encryption mandate — the clearest one I found anywhere in this research. But it obligates NIMC, as the party encoding data onto its own physical/digital ID card, to encrypt the prescribed part of that card's content. It says nothing about what a bank, MFB, or fintech must do when a customer later types their NIN into an onboarding form and the institution stores that number as a field in its own database. I found no provision anywhere in the NIMC Act that extends this encryption duty, or any other specific security-technology duty, to third-party relying institutions.

The NIMC Act does impose general confidentiality and security duties on NIMC itself regarding its own National Identity Database — Section 5(g) requires NIMC to "ensure the preservation, protection, sanctity and security (including cyber-security) of any information or data collected, obtained, maintained or stored in respect of the National Identity Database," and Section 26 restricts access to "the data or information contained in the Database" to cases authorised by NIMC or consented to by the individual, with an unconsented-disclosure carve-out for national security, crime prevention/detection, or purposes NIMC itself specifies by regulation. Both of these govern NIMC's own database, not a bank's copy of a customer's NIN once lawfully collected.

I searched specifically for a CBN circular that cross-references or adopts NIMC's data-protection rules for NIN handling by banks/MFBs and did not find one. This is an honest gap, not a confirmed "no" — I simply did not locate such a circular.

## Q6: If no clear, specific encryption mandate exists, what would "appropriate security measures proportionate to sensitivity" most defensibly mean for a system like GARBATI's? (Technical opinion, not a legal one)

This is my own reasoned technical read, offered because the task asked for it directly — not a legal conclusion, and not a substitute for compliance sign-off.

Even without a named-technology mandate, Section 39(1)'s risk factors point in one direction for BVN/NIN specifically. NDPC's own GAID (Schedule 4, the Data Privacy Impact Assessment template) defines what it calls an "ultimate or decisive credential" as "any information such as a password or a token. Once provided, it completes a transaction such as opening an email account, access to bank account or access to secure records" — and treats processing that involves such credentials as warranting at least a "POSSIBLE" (elevated) risk rating in its own DPIA scoring rubric, one tier above the "REMOTE" rating reserved for data "limited to nominal contact information... contains no sensitive personal data... and it contains no decisive or ultimate credential for authenticating valuable transactions." BVN and NIN are exactly the kind of identifier that, combined with other data points, enables identity theft, account takeover, and fraud in Nigeria's financial system — which is precisely the harm Section 39(1)(b) ("the nature, degree and likelihood of harm to a data subject") asks a controller to weigh.

Separately, Section 40(7)(a) — on how NDPC evaluates whether a breach is "likely to result in a risk to the rights and freedoms of a data subject" — explicitly lists "the likely effectiveness of any technical and administrative measures implemented to mitigate the likely harm resulting from the personal data breach, including any encryption or de-identification of the data" as a factor NDPC and the controller consider. The practical implication runs in reverse too: if BVN/NIN were stored in plaintext and a breach occurred, the absence of encryption would weigh toward the breach being treated as higher-risk, triggering the Act's stricter breach-notification and remediation obligations (direct data-subject notification "immediately," not just the 72-hour Commission notice) and likely toward a harsher view of whether "appropriate" measures were ever in place under Section 39(1) to begin with.

Put plainly: no specific law I found says "GARBATI, you must encrypt the BVN column." But the absence of that specific sentence does not mean plaintext storage of BVN/NIN in a production database would be defensible under the *general* duty that does exist, by any reasonable professional security standard or by NDPC's own stated risk-scoring logic. "No specific mandate" and "plaintext is fine" are not the same conclusion, and this research does not support the second one.

## On the owner's workplace storing BVN/NIN unencrypted

The task asked me to treat this honestly as evidence, not as the answer. I found nothing in NDPA, the GAID, the NIMC Act, or (to the extent I could read it) the CBN BVN framework that makes unencrypted BVN/NIN storage explicitly illegal in so many words — and I also found no NDPC enforcement action to date specifically punishing encryption failure. That is consistent with unencrypted storage being common in practice, including potentially at the owner's workplace. But "common" and "unenforced so far" are not the same as "compliant" or "safe" — NDPC states it is moving into "full enforcement mode" from 2026, the general Section 39(1) duty and the Section 24(1)(f)/24(2) principles (security, confidentiality, integrity) remain binding regardless of how many institutions currently fall short of them, and a professional reading of what "appropriate" means for a high-value identifier like BVN/NIN does not support plaintext storage. A qualified compliance officer, not this research, should be the one to tell the owner whether his workplace's practice is itself compliant — that was explicitly out of scope here.

## What I could not verify

This list is deliberately explicit — do not treat any of these as settled:

1. **Whether NDPC has, by any regulation or guidance notice separate from the GAID, formally prescribed BVN, NIN, or "financial data" generally as "sensitive personal data" under Section 30(2)/65(h) of the NDPA.** I read the full current GAID 2025 and found no such prescription in it, but I cannot rule out a separate NDPC instrument I didn't find.
2. **Whether the CBN "Revised Regulatory Framework for Bank Verification Number (BVN) Operations and Watch-List" (2021) contains its own encryption or security-specific clause.** Both of my attempts to extract readable text from this document (via two different tools, from two different PDF copies) failed — it appears to be an image-scan with no usable text layer available to me. This is the same gap the prior brief (`research/nigerian-regulatory-requirements.md`) already flagged; I was not able to close it with a different approach.
3. **Whether CBN has issued any circular that cross-references or adopts NIMC's internal NIN-handling/security rules for banks and MFBs.** Not found; not ruled out.
4. **Whether any NDPC enforcement action — past or in progress — has specifically cited inadequate encryption or lack of encryption as a violation basis**, as distinct from the lawful-basis/consent and cross-border-transfer violations I found in the two large fines I could confirm (Fidelity Bank, MultiChoice). I searched specifically for this and found none, but NDPC does not appear to publish a fully exhaustive public log of every enforcement action and its detailed factual basis that I could access, so this is an absence-of-evidence finding, not a confirmed absence-of-enforcement finding.
5. **Whether a court or NDPC adjudicatory decision has ever interpreted Section 39(1)'s "appropriate technical and organisational measures" standard to require encryption as a practical minimum for a specific category of data** (which would functionally convert the general standard into something closer to a specific mandate for that category, by precedent rather than by statutory text). I found no such case or decision; this is also consistent with NDPA being a young statute (2023) and GAID being newer still (2025), so case law may simply not yet exist.
6. **The GAID's "may ≠ merely advisory" interpretive note (Article 52)** is, on its own text, scoped to the GAID's own use of "may" — I did not find anything suggesting NDPC intends it to retroactively change how Section 39(2) of the Act itself ("measures... may include... encryption") should be read, but a compliance officer should confirm this reading is correct rather than relying on my inference.
