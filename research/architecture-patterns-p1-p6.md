## Plain-language summary (read this first)

A few terms used below, defined once:

- **Saga**: a way to keep a multi-step operation (that touches several services, each with its own database) consistent without a single database transaction across all of them. Instead of "all or nothing" at the database level, each step commits on its own, and if a later step fails, earlier steps are undone by deliberate "undo" steps (**compensating transactions**) written by the developer — the database does not do this automatically.
- **Orchestration** (one way to run a saga): one piece of code (an **orchestrator**) is in charge. It calls step 1, then step 2, then step 3, and decides what to do if any step fails. GARBATI's proposed `OrchestratorApi` is this role.
- **Choreography** (the other way to run a saga): there is no single piece of code in charge. Each service does its bit and then announces "I did X" as an event; other services listen for events that matter to them and react. Nobody has the whole picture in one place.
- **Policy/decision vs. execution separation**: one component decides a rule ("is this allowed", "what does this cost"), a different component actually carries out the effect (moves the money). This shows up elsewhere in software as **PDP/PEP** (Policy Decision Point / Policy Enforcement Point) — the "decider" and the "doer" are deliberately different pieces of code so the decision logic can change without touching the part that acts on it, and vice versa.
- **Database-per-service**: each service has its own database; no other service is allowed to query or join against it directly. The only way to get or combine data is to ask that service's API.
- **Modular monolith**: one deployed program (one process, one thing to run and pay for), but the code inside is organised into separate, clearly bounded modules — as if they were separate services — so that splitting them into real separate services later is (in principle) a matter of deployment, not a rewrite.
- **Monolith-first**: the advice (associated with Martin Fowler) to build a new system as a single deployable unit first, with clean internal boundaries, and only split pieces into separately deployed services once you have a concrete reason to.

GARBATI's P-1 to P-6 map onto five well-documented patterns: orchestration-based sagas (P-1), decision/execution separation as seen in core-banking ledger-vs-product-engine splits and in the general PDP/PEP idea (P-2, P-4), the scheduler role in multi-step workflow patterns (P-3), database-per-service (P-5), and modular-monolith/monolith-first (P-6). None of the six is an unusual or invented design; all six have direct, named counterparts in credible sources. Where sources disagree is on cost, not on whether the pattern exists — see "What this does not settle" at the end.

## Q1 (P-1): Is a dedicated orchestrator a recognized pattern? Orchestration vs. choreography trade-offs, and does banking/payments favor one?

**Short answer:** Yes. The **Saga pattern** is the standard named pattern for exactly this problem (a multi-step business operation spanning services that each keep their own data), and **orchestration** is one of its two standard implementation styles. Sources consistently describe orchestration as better suited to complex, conditional, auditable workflows — which matches a money-moving operation with authorization, pricing, and posting steps — while choreography suits simple, few-step, event-driven flows.

**Evidence:**
- [microservices.io — Pattern: Saga](https://microservices.io/patterns/data/saga.html) (Chris Richardson, the person who coined much of today's microservices pattern vocabulary): describes the saga as a sequence of local transactions, with orchestration and choreography as the two coordination styles, and names compensating transactions as the un-doing mechanism (not automatic — "developers must explicitly design" them).
- [Azure Architecture Center — Saga Design Pattern](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/patterns/saga) gives a direct comparison table. Choreography: "Good for simple workflows that have few services and don't need coordination logic" but "workflow can be confusing when you add new steps" and integration testing is hard. Orchestration: "Better suited for complex workflows or when you add new services," "avoids cyclic dependencies," "clear separation of responsibilities simplifies service logic" — at the cost of introducing the orchestrator as a point of failure and needing explicit coordination logic.
- [AWS Prescriptive Guidance — Saga orchestration patterns](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/prescriptive-guidance/latest/cloud-design-patterns/saga-patterns.html): recommends the saga (and orchestration specifically) when "a centralized view of the workflow is required, the system must support long-running transactions including timeouts and retries, and explicit compensation logic and robust observability are required" — this describes a banking transaction closely.
- No source found states outright "banking must use orchestration" — none of the sources make banking-specific claims about saga style. The inference that banking/payments favors orchestration is drawn by this researcher from the general guidance above (multi-step, conditional, needs audit trail and centralized retry/compensation logic), not a direct statement in any source about banking specifically. Treat that inference as reasonable, not as a cited industry consensus.

**Verdict — P-1: Matches common practice.** A dedicated orchestrator for a multi-step money operation is exactly the orchestration-based saga described by Richardson and by Azure's and AWS's architecture guidance; the reasons given in `ARCHITECTURE.md` (one place enforces order, ledger stays independent) line up with the named benefits of orchestration over choreography.

## Q2 (P-2 / P-4): Is separating "who decides the rules/price" from "who executes the money movement" a recognized pattern? Do real core-banking systems draw this boundary the same way?

**Short answer:** Yes, under a few different names, and real core-banking vendors draw a similar line, though not always identically.

**Evidence:**
- General pattern name: **Policy Decision Point / Policy Enforcement Point (PDP/PEP)**, formalized by the XACML standard (OASIS, 2003) for authorization, but the underlying idea — "differentiate between policy decision-making and policy enforcement, since enforcement is specific to a resource while a decision engine can be general-purpose and reusable" — is a general architectural separation, not limited to authorization. [Policy Decision Point — ScienceDirect overview](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/policy-decision-point); [Altinn docs on PDP/PEP architecture patterns](https://docs.altinn.studio/en/authorization/reference/system/application-architecture/authorization/patterns/).
- Core-banking-specific framing: [Formance — "Why modern fintechs layer a ledger under their core banking system"](https://www.formance.com/blog/industry-analysis/why-modern-fintechs-layer-a-ledger-under-their-core-banking-system) argues directly for this split: "the ledger should record the economics of a product precisely and understand none of its commercial rules," and names "letting the ledger absorb product logic" as a named failure mode — if special pricing/fee calculations creep into the posting engine, "a year later the ledger knows about every product, every product change needs a ledger release, and the bank has rebuilt the monolith it was trying to leave."
- [Fimple — Composable Banking Architecture](https://fimple.co.uk/composable-banking-architecture/) describes the same split in composable-banking vendor terms: a "product and pricing" component that owns "product definitions, rates, profit rates, fees, eligibility rules" but does not own balances or postings, feeding a separate ledger/posting layer.
- Real vendor examples: Temenos sells a standalone "Enterprise product service — a standalone product catalogue that enables banks to design and take to market products on a single system," separate from its transaction/ledger components ([Temenos press material, via search](https://www.temenos.com/press_release/temenos-reaches-major-milestone-with-the-roll-out-of-temenos-transact-microservices-architecture/)). Mambu is described as "a cloud-native SaaS core built around a product engine that lets non-engineers configure loan and deposit products," again separated from core transaction processing (per search-engine summaries of vendor marketing material; this researcher could not independently verify Mambu's or Temenos's internal service boundaries from primary technical documentation — vendor architecture internals are not publicly published in the same detail as `ARCHITECTURE.md`).

**What could not be verified:** Exactly how Temenos or Mambu technically enforce the "decide vs. execute" boundary inside their platforms (e.g., whether their product engine can bypass the posting engine in any workflow) is not documented in public, non-marketing sources this researcher could access. The comparison above rests on vendor-facing descriptions, not internal architecture docs or source code.

**Verdict — P-2: Matches common practice.** Splitting "who owns product/fee/rule definitions" from "who executes/posts" mirrors both the general PDP/PEP separation and the specific ledger-vs-product-engine split described for core banking and composable-banking vendors.

**Verdict — P-4: Matches common practice.** "Product computes, orchestrator posts (via Ledger)" is the same decide/execute split applied at the step level inside one transaction flow, consistent with the Formance warning against letting the posting engine (ledger) absorb pricing logic.

## Q3 (P-3): Is a separate scheduling/recurring-instruction service standard? How do systems structure retry and run-history for this?

**Short answer:** Yes — a separate component that triggers work on a schedule, distinct from the component that performs the work, is a named pattern (**Scheduler/Agent/Supervisor**), and it is standard for the Scheduler to keep its own durable run-history/state store with retry counts, separate from whatever executes the actual action.

**Evidence:**
- [Azure Architecture Center — Scheduler Agent Supervisor pattern](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/patterns/scheduler-agent-supervisor): the **Scheduler** "arranges the steps... and orchestrates their execution," recording state ("step not yet started," "running," "completed") in a durable **state store**; the **Agent** "encapsulates a call to a remote service... and implements error handling and retry logic"; the **Supervisor** "monitors the status of steps... and arranges for the appropriate Agent to recover the step" using a **retry count** against a threshold. This maps closely onto ScheduleApi (scheduler/supervisor role: fires the trigger, tracks run history, decides whether to retry) submitting to OrchestratorApi (which performs the actual multi-step work, effectively the agent/executor role).
- The same Azure page states step logic "must be idempotent" because the Supervisor may cause the same step to run more than once — this matches `ARCHITECTURE.md` 4.3's idempotency-key-per-run-date design directly.
- Banking-domain framing: a **standing order** is a customer-initiated, scheduled *push* payment, distinct from a **direct debit**, which is a payee-initiated pull; per [Wikipedia: Standing order (banking)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standing_order_(banking)) and [Modulr's explainer on direct debit](https://www.modulrfinance.com/blog-insights/what-is-direct-debit), banks typically do **not** automatically retry a failed standing order (the customer must sort it out), whereas direct debits can be re-presented for retry by the payee's bank. This is a real difference this researcher found between the two instruments — it is relevant if GARBATI's "standing orders" are meant to behave like true standing orders (fire-once-per-schedule, no silent retry) versus something closer to a direct debit (automatic re-presentation). `ARCHITECTURE.md` 4.3 does not yet specify retry behaviour beyond idempotency on a repeated trigger; whether a *failed* run (e.g., insufficient funds) is retried automatically or left for the customer to notice is an open design point, not something this research can resolve.

**What could not be verified:** No source was found describing a specific named "scheduling-service" pattern unique to banking standing orders (as opposed to the general Scheduler/Agent/Supervisor pattern and the standing-order-vs-direct-debit distinction above); this is general job-scheduling-triggers-workflow guidance applied to a banking case, not a banking-specific published pattern.

**Verdict — P-3: Matches common practice**, with one point worth double-checking: the real-world convention that standing orders are not auto-retried on failure (unlike direct debits) should inform ScheduleApi's retry design when it is built, so GARBATI's behaviour matches what "standing order" means to users.

## Q4 (P-5): Is "database-per-service, API calls only" standard? What are the costs vs. benefits, and does banking change the calculus?

**Short answer:** Yes, this is one of the most consistently repeated pieces of microservices guidance across every source checked. The costs are also consistently named: harder cross-service queries/reporting, no cross-service ACID transactions (so eventual consistency and sagas are needed instead), and more operational complexity from running multiple databases.

**Evidence:**
- [microservices.io — Pattern: Database per service](https://microservices.io/patterns/data/database-per-service.html): states the core benefit (loose coupling — "changes to one service's database does not impact any other service") and the core costs: implementing operations that span services is complicated because distributed transactions are problematic ("many modern (NoSQL) databases don't support them"), and cross-service queries require **API Composition** (the caller joins data itself by calling multiple APIs) or **CQRS** (maintaining a separate, synchronized read view) as workarounds.
- [Microsoft .NET architecture guide — Data sovereignty per microservice](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dotnet/architecture/microservices/architect-microservice-container-applications/data-sovereignty-per-microservice) (aimed at the same .NET audience as GARBATI's owner): names the same trade-off directly — a monolith's single database gives "ACID transactions and the SQL language... across all tables," letting you "easily write a query that combines data from multiple tables," while in microservices "you can't make a single ACID transaction across microservices... you must use eventual consistency when a business process spans multiple microservices. This is much harder to implement than simple SQL joins." This page explicitly ties the pattern to Domain-Driven Design's **Bounded Context** concept (Martin Fowler) — each service/bounded context owns its own model and data.
- [Azure Architecture Center — Saga pattern](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/patterns/saga) independently repeats the same point: database-per-service gives independent scaling and failure isolation but "complicates cross-service data consistency," which is exactly why the saga pattern (Q1) exists — the two patterns are designed to be used together.
- **Banking-specific angle:** no source made a banking-specific argument for or against database-per-service as a general rule. The one domain-relevant point found is in the Formance article (Q2 evidence): keeping the ledger as its own system of record, queried only through its own API, is presented as *protective* for reconciliation and audit (a single, append-only, authoritative source of truth per domain, not diluted by other services writing to or reading it directly) — which argues the pattern is at least as appropriate in banking as elsewhere, arguably more so given audit requirements, though this is this researcher's inference from the reconciliation argument, not a direct "banking needs database-per-service more than other industries" statement in any source.

**Verdict — P-5: Matches common practice.** Database-per-service with API-only access is the standard, named approach (Richardson/microservices.io, Microsoft, Azure all agree); the stated cost (harder cross-service queries and reporting, needing eventual consistency) is real and is the same cost GARBATI will face — nothing found suggests banking should be exempted from it, and the audit/reconciliation argument (one authoritative source of truth per domain) if anything supports it.

## Q5 (P-6): Is "design with clean boundaries, deploy fewer services at first" a recognized pattern? How hard is splitting a modular monolith later, really?

**Short answer:** Yes — this is Martin Fowler's **"Monolith First"** advice, and the practice of organizing a single deployable into well-bounded internal modules is commonly called a **modular monolith**. Sources agree the approach is sound as a starting point; they disagree (or are candid about uncertainty) on how easy the later split actually is.

**Evidence:**
- [Martin Fowler — bliki: MonolithFirst](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/MonolithFirst.html): "Almost all the successful microservice stories have started with a monolith that got too big and was broken up... almost all the cases where I've heard of a system that was built as a microservice system from scratch, it has ended up in serious trouble." He gives two reasons: (1) YAGNI / the "MicroservicePremium" — microservices carry real extra cost in a system's early life, when you don't yet know if the system will even be useful; (2) finding correct service boundaries is hard early on, and "any refactoring of functionality between services is much harder than it is in a monolith," so it is better to find boundaries inside a monolith first and split once they have proven stable. He is explicit about the limits of his own confidence: "I don't feel I have enough anecdotes yet to get a firm handle on how to decide whether to use a monolith-first strategy," and notes teams with prior microservices experience, or systems replacing an existing system with already-clear boundaries, may reasonably start with microservices instead.
- On the difficulty of the later split, Fowler is candid rather than reassuring: "You cannot assume that you can take an arbitrary system and break it into microservices" — most systems grow more interdependent over time, not less, which works against assuming a clean later split. He describes viable paths (gradually peeling off edge services, or eventually replacing the monolith as a "sacrificial architecture") but does not claim the split is cheap or guaranteed.
- Secondary sources on modular monoliths specifically (lower-tier but consistent with Fowler): summarized search results describe the modular monolith as "organized into independent, well-defined modules... based on domain-driven design principles," keeping "simplicity and ease of deployment" while aiming at an "easy split" later — but the same summary is candid that in practice "module boundaries often erode, resource isolation is absent, and the promised 'easy split' rarely happens." This researcher could not independently verify the specific source of that caveat (it came from an AI-generated search summary citing several blog posts, not one identified, checkable primary source) and flags it as a claim to treat cautiously rather than as a confirmed, sourced fact — it matches Fowler's own caution above, but is not from Fowler directly.

**What this means for GARBATI specifically:** `ARCHITECTURE.md` Section 7 already tries to reduce this risk by keeping ProductApi and OrchestratorApi as **separate projects inside one deployable with the same boundaries enforced in code** (not just "modules in one project"), and calling for the LedgerApi/IdentityApi database-per-service rule (P-5) to hold even while they are not separately deployed. That is a stronger starting position than a generic modular monolith, because it is explicitly trying to keep the "no shared database, API-shaped calls only" discipline (P-5) even inside the single deployable — which, per Fowler's own warning, is exactly the discipline that most determines whether a later split is actually easy or not. No source directly confirms this specific combination ("project-per-boundary plus enforced no-shared-DB, inside one deployable") will make the later split easy — that is this researcher's reasoning from the general sources, not a tested claim from any of them.

**Verdict — P-6: Matches common practice**, with the caveat (per Fowler himself) that the ease of the later split is not guaranteed by any source checked — it depends on actually keeping the internal boundaries and the no-shared-database discipline intact in practice, which is a discipline question for whoever builds it, not something the plan on paper can guarantee by itself.
