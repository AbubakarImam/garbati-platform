# Dual-control / maker-checker / k-of-N approval patterns, for GARBATI joint accounts

**Task:** T-014. **Status:** Research only — this is not a decision and does not change any code or design document. It exists to inform the owner's upcoming decision on IdentityApi's joint-account design (the design-draft ratification tracked as D-9 in `research/identityapi-design-review.md`, and the related open items in `ARCHITECTURE.md` Section 9 around P-12/the decision-token design).

**What this extends:** `Garbati_IdentityApi_Design.md` Section 2.2 currently assumes **one active mandate per account** — IdentityApi checks that one mandate, then issues a signed grant (Section 3). The owner has decided v1 will support **joint accounts**: each holder gets their own mandate on the same account, and the account carries a **configurable required-approval count** (e.g. "any 1 of 2," "both of 2," "any 2 of 3"). That turns "check one mandate, issue a grant" into "collect enough approvals from enough holders, then issue a grant" — a new, stateful, multi-party flow, with its own timeout question separate from the existing 30-second single-grant expiry (P-12).

---

## Plain-language summary (read this first)

A few terms, defined once, in the order you'll meet them:

- **Dual control**: a rule that a sensitive action needs **two different people** to happen — one person alone can never do it. Common in banking for anything that moves money or changes who can move money.
- **Maker-checker** (also called "4-eyes"): the specific banking/treasury version of dual control. One person (the **maker**) creates or requests a transaction; a **different** person (the **checker**) reviews and approves it before it actually happens. The maker is not allowed to also be the checker of their own work.
- **M-of-N** (also written **k-of-N**, "threshold approval"): a rule that any **M** out of a total of **N** authorized people must agree before an action proceeds — not necessarily the *same* M each time, just *some* M of them. "2-of-3" means any two of three people.
- **Multisig** ("multi-signature"): the cryptocurrency-wallet version of M-of-N. A wallet is set up with N authorized keys (often held by different people or devices); a transaction needs signatures from at least M of them before it can move funds. The best-known production implementation is **Safe** (formerly Gnosis Safe).
- **Threshold**: the required count (M) itself — e.g. "the threshold is 2" means 2 approvals are needed, regardless of N.
- **Weighted voting**: a variant where approvers don't count equally — each approver has a "weight," and the *sum* of weights of those who approved must clear a threshold, rather than a simple head-count.
- **Quorum**: a general term (not specific to banking or crypto) for "the minimum number of participants required before a decision counts."
- **Nonce**: a number that increases by one for each new transaction a system processes, used so that a signature/approval tied to one specific transaction can never be reused ("replayed") for a different, later transaction.

**Short answer to the five questions below:** "Dual control," "maker-checker," and "M-of-N/multisig" are the same underlying idea (a count threshold on independent approvers) wearing three different domain costumes, with one real difference worth keeping: maker-checker usually also enforces a **role split** (the person who *requested* the action cannot be the person who *approves* it), while plain M-of-N multisig does not — any of the N keyholders can contribute any of the M signatures, with no requirement that the requester be excluded. A simple, unweighted "any M of N" count is the standard default shape everywhere this was checked; weighted votes and named-required-approvers exist but are treated as an added-complexity variant, not the default. Real systems vary enormously on pending-approval timeout — from an "indefinite" banking norm with soft overdue flags, to multi-day enterprise escalation windows, to minutes-long identity-verification popups — none of which resemble GARBATI's existing 30-second machine-to-machine grant, because that number assumes no human is in the loop. Reconfiguring a threshold is the one place the two domains disagree in a way that matters: multisig systems (Safe) let the **current** threshold's worth of signers approve a reconfiguration, while real banking joint-account mandate practice, as documented in multiple sources, requires **every** current holder's consent to change the mandate, not just enough of them to meet today's threshold. Partial approvals are built to be single-use and transaction-specific in the correct/standard design (never reusable across different requests) — but there is a documented, real implementation hazard where a removed signer's already-given approval can wrongly keep counting toward a transaction if the system doesn't explicitly re-validate the pending queue when membership changes.

## Q1: Named pattern(s) — is this the same thing across domains?

**Short answer:** yes, structurally the same pattern (count-threshold approval by independent parties), described differently per domain, with one real difference: maker-checker typically bundles in a role split that bare M-of-N multisig does not.

- **Banking/treasury: "maker-checker" / "dual control" / "4-eyes."** Maker-checker is described as "one of the central principles of authorization in the information systems of financial organizations," requiring "at least two individuals" for each transaction's completion — one person (maker) creates it, a separate person (checker) confirms/authorizes it. [Wikipedia: Maker-checker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maker-checker); [faisalkhan.com Payments Wiki: Maker-Checker](https://faisalkhan.com/knowledge-center/payments-wiki/m/maker-checker). "Dual control" is the broader banking term for the same mechanism, defined by one source as "a governance pattern that requires two independent authorised people to approve a sensitive action before it is released," explicitly narrower than general "segregation of duties," which is about dividing whole job *functions*, not one specific action. [nhimg.org: Dual Control](https://nhimg.org/glossary/dual-control/). A real bank's own documentation states the rule plainly: "dual control, where the initiator cannot approve their own work" — South Shore Bank, Business Banking Payments Approval guide (PDF, retrieved via WebFetch in this research). FFIEC's BSA/AML examination manual recommends "incorporating dual controls and the segregation of duties to the extent possible" as part of internal controls. [FFIEC BSA/AML Manual — Internal Controls](https://bsaaml.ffiec.gov/manual/AssessingTheBSAAMLComplianceProgram/02).
- **Multisig cryptocurrency wallets: "M-of-N."** "N represents the total number of keys generated and distributed, while M represents the minimum number of signatures required to approve a transaction" — e.g. 2-of-3 needs two of three keyholders. [Chainlink: Multi-Signature Wallets](https://chain.link/article/multi-signature-wallet); [dev.to: Multisig Wallets Explained](https://dev.to/basisdesk/multisig-wallets-explained-how-m-of-n-security-works-1c8c). The leading production implementation, **Safe** (formerly Gnosis Safe), stores "a threshold — the minimum number of owner approvals required to execute a transaction" and treats every owner symmetrically: any of the owners can contribute any of the required signatures. [Safe Help Center: why transactions with the same nonce conflict](https://help.safe.global/en/articles/40839-why-are-transactions-with-the-same-nonce-conflicting-with-each-other); summarized via secondary technical write-ups of Safe's contract design (e.g. [Medium: Gnosis Safe Internals — Signing Transactions](https://medium.com/@cizeon/gnosis-safe-internals-part-3-signing-transactions-93fcced50a29)). Multisig does **not** inherently require that the person who proposed a transaction be excluded from signing it — that's a maker-checker-specific refinement, not a feature of bare M-of-N.
- **Enterprise payment-approval software: "approval workflow" / "dual approval" / "approval matrix."** Products such as Ramp, SAP Concur and treasury-management platforms describe the same count-threshold idea using workflow language — "dual approval," tiered dollar thresholds (e.g. "$1,000–$10,000: both the manager and finance team must approve"), and sometimes *sequential* approval where "each approver [is] assigned a level that determines the order of review." [Ramp: Payment Approval Process](https://ramp.com/blog/payment-approval-process); [SAP Concur Community: Workflow](https://community.concur.com/marav43842/attachments/marav43842/ConcurExpense/32457/1/EXP_SG_Workflow_AuthAppr.pdf). A real treasury-ops article frames bank-grade payment approval as explicitly "dual control... the initiator cannot approve their own work," the same maker-checker language used in retail banking. [XTransfer: Architecting a Secure Withdrawal Process Approval Workflow](https://www.xtransfer.com/newsroom/architecting-a-secure-withdrawal-process-approval-workflow-for-multiuser-accounts).
- **Does the difference matter for GARBATI?** Yes, potentially. If GARBATI ever lets a joint-account holder both *initiate* a debit request and count as one of their own required approvers, that is the one point where "plain M-of-N" and "maker-checker" diverge in practice — several real systems (the bank PDF above, dual-control definitions) treat "the initiator cannot approve their own work" as a hard rule, not an optional nicety. This is listed as an open point in the recommendation section below, because the task's own framing ("any 1 of 2," "both of 2") does not yet say whether the initiating holder is automatically counted as one of the approvals or must be a separate, additional approver.

**What I could not verify:** no single source states "maker-checker and M-of-N are formally the same named pattern" — that equivalence is this researcher's structural comparison across the three domain-specific descriptions found, not a claim made by any cited source itself.

## Q2: Threshold semantics — simple count, or something more?

**Short answer:** an unordered, unweighted "any M of N" count is the standard, simplest, documented default across all three domains. Weighted votes and named-required-approvers exist as real, documented variants, but are consistently framed as the more complex option, not the default.

- **Banking joint-account mandates** use three named, standard mandate types: **"any one to sign"** (any single named signatory can act alone), **"all to sign"** (every named signatory must sign), and **"any two to sign"** (a defined number must act together) — all unordered, unweighted counts over a fixed list of named people. [Bank Ombudsman NZ: Account mandates](https://www.bankomb.org.nz/guides-and-cases/quick-guides/bank-accounts/account-mandates); [Cygnetise: Types of authorized signatories](https://www.cygnetise.com/blog/2-2020-types-of-authorized-signatories); [Creodata: Authorized Signatories & Signing Mandates](https://www.creodata.com/blog/authorized-signatories-signing-mandates/). None of the sources found describe a banking joint-account mandate type that requires one *specific* named individual (e.g. "must include the eldest holder") as a standard, commonly offered product — the three named types above (one, all, any-N) are presented as the standard menu.
- **Multisig** defaults to unweighted M-of-N. A documented variant, **"threshold multisig,"** assigns each signer a weight, and the *sum* of weights of those who approved must clear a predefined threshold rather than a simple headcount — used, for example, to give some signers more voting power than others. [dev.to: Multisig Wallets Explained](https://dev.to/basisdesk/multisig-wallets-explained-how-m-of-n-security-works-1c8c). The same source frames the security/usability trade-off directly: "lower thresholds (like 1-of-3) prioritize availability... higher thresholds (like 3-of-3) maximize security but create fragility: losing any single key means funds are permanently inaccessible" — i.e. the standard warning is not against complexity in the abstract, but specifically against setting the threshold too low (weak control) or too high/all-must-sign (one lost or unavailable holder can permanently lock the account).
- **Enterprise payment approval** supports both: simple count thresholds (dollar-tiered "two approvers required above $X") and more complex sequential/level-based routing where approvers act in a defined order, or a "dynamic condition-based matrix" that routes to specific escalated approvers (e.g. a named regional CFO) based on transaction attributes like currency or destination country. [Ramp: Payment Approval Process](https://ramp.com/blog/payment-approval-process); [XTransfer](https://www.xtransfer.com/newsroom/architecting-a-secure-withdrawal-process-approval-workflow-for-multiuser-accounts); [SAP Concur Community: Workflow](https://community.concur.com/marav43842/attachments/marav43842/ConcurExpense/32457/1/EXP_SG_Workflow_AuthAppr.pdf).
- **No source directly states** "use the simple count as your default and avoid the rest" as an explicit recommendation sentence — that conclusion is this researcher's synthesis of (a) the three domains all *offering* simple count as their named, standard option, and (b) the multisig source's explicit fragility warning about the *extremes* of the threshold range, not about complexity for its own sake.

**What I could not verify:** no source gives a general, cross-domain "best practice" statement recommending against weighted/named-approver schemes specifically; the "simplest safe default" conclusion above is inferred from what each domain treats as its *standard offering*, not from an explicit warning against the alternatives.

## Q3: Pending-approval timeout — what's the real-world window, and what happens on timeout?

**Short answer:** real systems vary from "indefinite" (banking) to multi-day escalation windows (enterprise) to minutes-long identity-verification popups (banking, for a *different* sub-step) — none of them resemble a 30-second machine grant, because 30 seconds assumes no human needs to notice, read and act. On timeout, the standard pattern is an explicit status change (e.g. "Overdue," "Past Due"), not silent disappearance.

- **A real bank's documented behavior** (South Shore Bank's Business Banking "Payments Approval" guide, a live digital-banking-platform FAQ, read in full via this research): "**How long do payments stay here awaiting approval? A: Indefinitely.**" There is no hard expiry on the approval request itself. Separately, a *recurring* payment is only queued for approval "2 business days before the send date," and if a wire requiring two approvals gets its first approval but not its second by day 2, it moves to an **"Overdue"** status rather than vanishing — it stays actionable, just flagged. Approving something after its intended value date is explicitly allowed, producing a **"Past Due"** warning rather than a rejection. (South Shore Bank, "Payments Approval," Business Banking guide PDF, pages 1 and 5, fetched directly in this research — not independently re-hosted at a stable public URL beyond the bank's own site.)
- **The same document separately describes a short, human-presence-oriented timeout** for a *different* step — the out-of-band identity-verification popup shown when a human actually clicks "approve": "the approval pop-up window times out after **5 minutes**." And identity-verification "caching" (skipping re-verification for a few minutes after the first one in a session) is capped at "5 minutes or less — set by the FI." These are the closest real numbers to "a human is actively, momentarily present," as distinct from "a request is sitting waiting for someone to notice it."
- **Enterprise/general workflow tooling** commonly recommends **48-hour** escalation windows before a pending approval auto-routes to a backup approver. [Virtosoftware: Payment Approval Workflow](https://www.virtosoftware.com/business-workflows/payment-approval-workflow/); [Ramp: Payment Approval Process](https://ramp.com/blog/payment-approval-process). Generic workflow *platforms* (not banking-specific) impose their own hard technical caps unrelated to policy — e.g. GitHub Actions environment-protection approvals and Microsoft Power Automate approvals both time out automatically at **28–30 days**. [Power Automate 30-day limit discussion](https://www.matthewdevaney.com/extend-a-power-automate-approval-over-the-30-day-limit/). These are platform ceilings, not evidence of what a correct banking approval window should be.
- **On what happens to a timed-out request**, the one concrete example found (South Shore Bank) shows it does **not** silently vanish or require a fully fresh start — it gets a visible terminal/warning status ("Overdue," "Past Due") and can still be approved afterward, with the system warning that it is late. This matches the general audit-everything expectation already established for GARBATI (`research/identityapi-design-review.md`), but is not the same shape as "expired, must start completely over," which is also a legitimate documented pattern elsewhere (e.g. GARBATI's own existing 30-second grant, which the IdentityApi draft treats as dead on expiry, requiring a fresh authorization request rather than a late-arriving approval).
- **Re-validation at the moment of execution, not just at approval time**, is explicitly confirmed by the same bank document: "Before approval, each payment must pass a limits and entitlements check; if the initiator has exceeded their limits or **no longer has the necessary permission**, the payment fails" — i.e. the system re-checks the initiator's live eligibility immediately before honoring the approval, rather than trusting that eligibility checked earlier is still valid. This is a close, concrete real-world precedent for GARBATI's own "no stale-approval caching" rule (`research/identityapi-design-review.md`), applied here to the *initiator's* standing rather than the approvers', but the same principle.

**What I could not verify:** no Nigerian or core-banking-vendor-specific (Temenos, Finacle) numeric pending-approval timeout was found despite targeted searching — vendor documentation for these platforms was not accessible through the sources searched. No source gives a single "correct" number for a human multi-party approval window generally; the range above (minutes to days to "indefinite") is the real spread found, not a converging standard.

## Q4: Reconfiguration safeguards — who can change the threshold, and does changing it need its own approval?

**Short answer:** both domains require that a threshold change go through an approval gate of its own — but they disagree on *whose* approval. Multisig requires only the **current threshold's worth** of signers; real banking mandate practice, as documented, requires **every** current holder's consent. This is the one place the two domains meaningfully diverge, and it matters directly to the scenario the task asks about (one holder unilaterally weakening the account).

- **Multisig (Safe):** "Changing either the owner list or threshold requires a Safe transaction signed by enough of the current owners to meet the current threshold, so a Safe cannot be taken over by adding an owner without the existing owners' consent." Concretely: in a 2-of-3 Safe, any 2 of the 3 current owners can together approve lowering the threshold to 1-of-3 — the *third* owner's consent is not required, only enough owners to clear today's bar. [Safe documentation, summarized across multiple independent technical write-ups: LogRocket — Build a treasury wallet with multisignature Gnosis Safe](https://blog.logrocket.com/build-treasury-wallet-multisignature-gnosis-safe/); corroborated by secondary sources describing the same mechanism (TRM Labs, GoldRush, MT Global guides to Safe setup/administration).
- **Real-world banking joint-account mandates are documented as stricter.** Multiple independent sources describe mandate changes as requiring **unanimous** consent of every current account holder, not merely those currently empowered to act: "the mandate for operating the account can be defined/modified with the consent of all account holders only" [Bank Ombudsman NZ: Account mandates](https://www.bankomb.org.nz/guides-and-cases/quick-guides/bank-accounts/account-mandates); a bank's own customer-facing terms state the same ("if you... want to switch from one [mandate] type to another... you may need the agreement of all account holders"); and for a comparable structure (a partnership firm's bank account), the explicit rule cited is that "any change in the operation style or mandate... will need the approval and signatures of **ALL THE partners and NOT JUST** the active signatory" ([general partnership-account mandate-change guidance, cross-checked against multiple banking-practice summaries found in this search](https://www.co-operativebank.co.uk/pdfs/bank/business/customerservices/managingyouraccount/change-of-signatories-and-authorised-users.pdf)).
- **Why the difference matters for GARBATI specifically:** in a 2-of-3 GARBATI joint account under the Safe-style rule, two of the three holders could together lower the threshold to 1-of-3 without the third holder's knowledge or consent, after which either of those two could unilaterally debit the account — exactly the "weaken the account's protection without the others' consent" scenario the task is asking about. Under the banking-mandate-style rule, that same change would require all three holders' consent, including the one whose protection is being reduced, closing that gap. No source found states one of these two approaches is objectively "more correct" — they are two real, each independently well-documented, patterns that produce different outcomes in exactly the scenario this task cares about.

**What I could not verify:** no source was found specifically discussing *why* Safe chose "current threshold" over "all owners" for its own reconfiguration rule (i.e. no design-rationale document, only the observed behavior); no CBN-specific or Nigerian-banking-specific rule on joint-account mandate-change consent was found — see "What this does not settle" below.

## Q5: Partial-approval failure modes — what happens if a holder is suspended/revoked mid-approval, and can approvals be reused?

**Short answer:** in the correct/standard design, an approval is single-use and bound to one exact pending request — it cannot be reused for a different request, which matches GARBATI's existing no-replay philosophy directly. What happens to an *already-given* approval when the approving holder is later suspended, revoked, or dies before the threshold is met is less clearly standardized: the strongest, most concrete evidence found is (a) a documented implementation *hazard* in multisig systems where naive logic can wrongly keep counting a removed signer's old approval, which the literature treats as a bug to guard against, not a feature; and (b) a real bank's confirmed practice of re-validating the *initiator's* live standing immediately before honoring any approval, rather than trusting an earlier check.

- **Reuse across requests — not standard, by design.** In Safe/multisig, each signature is computed over a transaction hash that bakes in the exact transaction details **and a sequential nonce specifically to prevent replay** — "the transaction hash incorporates... nonce... to prevent replay attacks," and "Safe uses a sequential nonce starting at 0, and skipping a nonce blocks all subsequent transactions." [Safe Help Center: Why are transactions with the same nonce conflicting with each other?](https://help.safe.global/en/articles/40839-why-are-transactions-with-the-same-nonce-conflicting-with-each-other). This means a holder's signature on one transaction has zero validity for any other transaction, even one for the identical amount and recipient — a fresh approval is always required per request. This matches GARBATI's existing single-use/replay rule for the single-holder grant almost exactly (`Garbati_IdentityApi_Design.md` Section 3.7; ARCHITECTURE.md P-12), just applied one layer earlier, to the human approval step rather than the machine-issued grant.
- **A documented real hazard, not a sanctioned behavior:** multiple independent technical sources (a GitHub issue discussion on a Safe-pattern contract implementation, and a separate developer-support thread) describe a known class of bug where, if a system does not explicitly re-validate pending approvals when the owner set changes, "pending confirmations from a removed owner would still count toward [the] threshold, and a new owner could confirm a transaction they never saw" — i.e. a removed holder's stale partial approval can wrongly keep counting unless the implementation deliberately guards against it. One source frames the fix as owner rotation that is "safe by construction against the pending-queue." This is strong, specific, directly-relevant evidence that **the correct design must explicitly invalidate or re-check a holder's outstanding partial approvals when that holder's standing changes** — leaving it unhandled is a documented, named failure mode, not a neutral default.
- **Re-validation at execution time, for the initiator at least, is a confirmed real banking practice:** "Before approval, each payment must pass a limits and entitlements check; if the initiator has exceeded their limits or no longer has the necessary permission, the payment fails" (South Shore Bank Payments Approval guide, as cited under Q3). This confirms the general principle — don't trust that eligibility checked earlier is still true — but the source found addresses the *initiator's* standing specifically, not explicitly a scenario where one of several *approvers'* standing changes mid-process.
- **Delegation/reassignment is a distinct, better-documented pattern,** addressing a related but different problem: what happens when an approver is simply *unavailable* (on leave, busy) rather than suspended/revoked. Enterprise systems commonly let a pending *approval task* be reassigned or delegated to someone else — "any approver can reassign the approval to a different approver," or a user can pre-configure a delegate for a known absence window. [Oracle Fusion: How Payment Approval Is Processed](https://docs.oracle.com/en/cloud/saas/financials/26b/fappp/how-payment-approval-is-processed.html); [Sage Intacct: Delegate bill approvals when an approver is out of office](https://www.intacct.com/ia/docs/en_US/releasenotes/2025/2025_Release_3/Accounts_Payable/2025-R3-ap-approval-delegation.htm). This reassigns who may give a *new* approval; it is a different mechanism from the question of whether an *already-given* approval should remain valid.

**What I could not verify:** no source was found specifically describing standard banking maker-checker practice for "a maker's or checker's partial action is suspended/revoked/the person dies before the other half completes" — the FFIEC/dual-control sources found describe the principle (two independent people) but not this specific mid-process failure scenario. The multisig evidence on this point (Q5's strongest finding) comes from implementation-level discussion (GitHub issues/support threads describing a known hazard class), not from a standards body or an official Safe security document stating the correct behavior as policy — treat it as strong circumstantial evidence of a real, named hazard, not as a cited "must do X" standard.

## Recommended default shape for GARBATI

**This is a recommendation for the owner to accept, change, or reject — it is not a decision this research makes, and it does not resolve any "human-only gate" item under `AGENTS.md` (joint accounts change authorization rules, which is explicitly one of those gates).**

1. **Threshold semantics:** default to a simple, unordered, unweighted "any M of N" count — matching the task's own framing ("any 1 of 2," "both of 2," "any 2 of 3") and the standard, simplest option found in all three domains checked (banking mandate types, bare multisig, basic enterprise approval tiers). Do not add weighted votes or a named-required-approver rule in v1; both exist as real, documented patterns but add state, testing surface and explanation burden with no evidence found that GARBATI needs them yet. **Open point to resolve explicitly, not left to inference:** decide whether the holder who *initiates* a debit request is automatically counted as one of the M required approvals, or must be excluded and get a separate approval from the remaining holders — real systems differ (maker-checker commonly excludes the initiator; bare multisig does not), and the task's own wording doesn't settle this.
2. **Pending-approval timeout range:** recommend something in the **low single-digit minutes to roughly one hour** as a starting range for GARBATI's human-in-the-loop joint-approval window — well above the 30-second machine grant (a human must notice a request, open a channel, and act) but well below banking's "indefinite" reference pattern or enterprise's 48-hour/multi-day escalation windows, because GARBATI's joint approval sits **in front of** a live, customer-initiated debit decision rather than behind a scheduled payment with a flexible value date — the closer real-world analogue is "is a human actively present and about to act" (the 5-minute OOB popup/caching window found in the South Shore Bank example) than "a payment queue someone will get to eventually." On timeout: recommend an explicit, durable, auditable **"Expired"** state be recorded for the joint-approval request (not silent disappearance, and not an indefinite "Overdue" carry-forward as banking payment queues do) — matching GARBATI's existing audit-everything and no-stale-caching rules, and requiring any late approval after expiry to start a fresh approval request rather than being honored retroactively.
3. **Reconfiguration safeguard:** recommend requiring consent from **every current holder** (not merely enough holders to meet the *current* threshold) before a joint account's required-approval count can change — the stricter of the two real patterns found, matching documented real-world banking joint-account mandate-change practice rather than the Safe/multisig pattern. This directly closes the scenario the task itself names: two of three holders in a 2-of-3 account should not be able to unilaterally lower the account to 1-of-3 without the third holder's knowledge, under this recommendation.
4. **Partial-approval validity on a holder's status change:** recommend treating a holder's already-given partial approval as **void, not carried forward,** the moment that holder's mandate is suspended or revoked (matching the hazard specifically documented in multisig implementation discussions — a removed signer's stale approval should not silently keep counting), and recommend an explicit live re-check of every remaining approver's current standing at the moment the threshold is reached and the grant would be issued (not just at the moment each individual approval was given) — mirroring the confirmed real banking practice of re-validating the initiator's standing immediately before honoring an approval, extended here to every party in the approval, not just the initiator.
5. **Single-use, no reuse across requests:** recommend each joint-approval request get a request-specific identifier a holder's approval is cryptographically or at minimum database-uniquely bound to (the multisig nonce-binding pattern), so an approval given for one debit can never be mistaken for, or replayed against, a different debit — this is a direct extension of the no-replay rule GARBATI has already established for the single-holder grant (`research/identityapi-design-review.md`), not a new principle.

## What this does not settle

- **Does not decide anything.** This is research feeding the owner's and coordinator's decision, not a decision itself.
- **Not a Sharia ruling.** One relevant item was found and must be flagged, not treated as settled: AAOIFI's Sharia standards describe a joint account as one where holders "shall have equal rights and equal obligations unless the joint account holders agree otherwise," that "the balance of the joint account shall be considered as owned equally by holders... unless otherwise agreed," and that "account holders must specify the authorities of the joint signatories." [AAOIFI Shari'ah Standards, 2015 edition (PDF)](https://www.iefpedia.com/english/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/Shariaa-Standards-ENG.pdf); general background on joint/shared-ownership structures in Islamic finance (musharakah) describing profit-and-loss sharing proportional to contribution. [See also general musharakah background](https://cbonds.com/glossary/musharaka/). This is background context only — **whether a particular approval-threshold design (e.g. a 1-of-N veto-style threshold, an all-must-agree threshold, or something else) is required, permitted, or disallowed for a Sharia-compliant GARBATI joint account is a question for a qualified Sharia scholar/board, not something this research determines.** Equal-ownership-by-default framing in AAOIFI's standard may have implications for whether a disproportionate approval power (e.g. one holder able to act alone, or weighted votes) is appropriate — flagging this as a question to put in front of the Sharia board, not resolving it here.
- **Not a Nigerian regulatory ruling.** CBN's Customer Due Diligence Regulations and Global Standing Instruction (GSI) guidelines require identifying and obtaining evidence for **all** signatories to a joint account, and executing GSI mandates with **all** signatories' execution. [CBN GSI Operational Guidelines — Individuals (PDF)](https://www.cbn.gov.ng/Out/2020/CCD/CBN%20-%20Operational%20Guidelines%20on%20Global%20Standing%20Instructions%20GSI%20-%20Individuals.pdf). No CBN-specific rule on joint-account **approval-threshold mechanics** (how many of how many holders must approve a debit, or how reconfiguration consent should work) was found in this research. A qualified compliance officer must confirm whether CBN or any other Nigerian regulator has specific requirements here before this is built; see also the existing gaps already flagged in `research/nigerian-regulatory-requirements.md`.
- **Does not replace the owner's product judgment.** Nothing here determines whether GARBATI actually needs weighted votes, named-required-approvers, or anything beyond the simple count-threshold recommended above — that is a product decision informed by, not dictated by, this research.
